# Decisions belong in streams, not results

No canonical Domain-Driven Design text says "attach provenance to the entity" or "always separate it". The closest thing to doctrine, Evans's own *DDD Reference*, ranks three options and puts the **why, who and when of a change on an immutable Domain Event that refers to entities by identity**, not on the entity ([Evans, DDD Reference](https://domainlanguage.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/DDD_Reference_2015-03.pdf)). Shipping software agrees. Event stores put provenance as metadata on each event. Audit frameworks keep history in separate tables. Lineage catalogs model the producing run as its own entity. Cloud providers and authorization engines keep decision logs in a plane the resource owner cannot edit. The one widely used pattern that sits on the entity (Spring Data's `@CreatedBy`/`@LastModifiedBy`, or a jsonb `metadata` bag) holds only *current* provenance and is lost when the row is deleted. Denials are where attachment fails outright: a refused action produces no result to attach anything to. No row-history tool surveyed records a rejected write, and every system that does record denials (CloudTrail, Google's Policy Denied logs, Entra, PCI DSS-driven logging) keeps them in a separate log. The operator's intuition, "provenance is always attached to the thing that needs provenance", is right for **derivation provenance** ("this result came from that run"), and that link should live on the result. It is wrong for **decision records**: a decision exists before its result, can exist with no result at all, can cover several results, and must be trusted independently of the record it governs. For beep-effect's execution-authority ledger, the evidence favors keeping the decision stream separate and append-only, keyed by session, with the Session's provenance partition carrying a pointer to the decision. It does not favor collapsing the ledger into that partition and sending denials to telemetry. The decision rule at the end is my synthesis of the sources. It is not doctrine any of them states.

## Evans and Young put a change's provenance on the event, not the entity

Evans's "Domain Events" pattern is the most direct canonical statement. It explains why neither existing option is enough on its own: "Audit trails can allow tracing, but are not usually suited to being used for the logic of the program itself. Change histories of entities can allow access to previous states, but ignores the meaning of those changes." His remedy is to "represent each event as a domain object". That object is **immutable**, carries "the identity of entities involved", and has two timestamps, one for when the event occurred and one for when it was entered, plus "the identity of the person who entered it" ([Evans, DDD Reference p.12](https://domainlanguage.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/DDD_Reference_2015-03.pdf)). That is a bitemporal provenance record living next to the entity, not inside it. Domain Events were not in the 2003 book. Evans added them later, so the "Blue Book" itself is silent on this question.

Greg Young's *CQRS Documents* says where the audit context goes. His events table is keyed by aggregate, and he notes that "most organizations would want to add a few columns" for context: "the user that initiated the change, the ip address they sourced the change from, or **their level of permission** when they sourced the change" ([Young, CQRS Documents](https://cqrs.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/11/cqrs_documents.pdf)). This is the clearest canonical precedent for storing authorization context with each change record. It belongs to the event row in an aggregate's stream, not to a field on the aggregate. Young's accounting-ledger argument explains why this matters: the current balance "can be trusted because... the transactions from the 'beginning of time'... could be re-run", so "there exists a verifiable audit log". Fowler's catalogue gives the cost-based version of the same choice. Put temporal data on the object (Effectivity, Temporal Property) when domain logic reads history as part of normal processing, accepting that these patterns "add a lot of complexity to an object model". Use a separate Audit Log when humans read history rarely: it is "easy to write but harder to read" ([Fowler, Audit Log](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/AuditLog.html); [Temporal Property](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/TemporalProperty.html)). Fowler also observes that a payroll run which **records the inputs it used** makes a separate bitemporal model unnecessary for audit ([Fowler, Bitemporal History](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)). That point applies directly to decision records that capture the grant set they were evaluated against.

W3C PROV supports both placements and makes the separate one first-class. Its core model splits Entity, Activity and Agent into distinct node types. One activity can use and generate many entities ([PROV-DM](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/)), so flattening an activity into attributes of one output would duplicate it across every output and lose its inputs. Provenance can be "published or embed[ded] alongside the data". It can also live at its own provenance-URI, reached from the resource by a `prov:has_provenance` link ([PROV-AQ](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-aq/)). A provenance **Bundle** "is itself an entity, so allowing provenance of provenance" ([PROV-DM §2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/)). The DDD Domain Event and the PROV Activity are close to isomorphic. That observation is mine, not something either spec says.

Two gaps should be stated plainly. Vernon's *IDDD* could not be checked: the only evidence is a search snippet saying change tracking is best done "with Domain Events and an Event Store". And no canonical author classes audit as a generic subdomain, a supporting subdomain or a bounded context of its own.

## Shipping software attaches only "who touched it last"

Libraries consistently put **current** provenance on the row and **history** beside it. Spring Data JPA's `@CreatedBy`/`@LastModifiedBy` fields sit on the entity, are overwritten on every update, and keep no history; for history the docs point to Hibernate Envers ([Spring Data JPA Auditing](https://docs.spring.io/spring-data/jpa/reference/auditing.html)). Every history-keeping library uses a separate table. PaperTrail uses a generic `versions` table, and its extra provenance has to be **real columns** there, not keys in a blob ([paper_trail](https://github.com/paper-trail-gem/paper_trail)). Audited, django-simple-history (one shadow table per model) and django-auditlog follow the same pattern. django-auditlog's `delete_related` defaults to False "to keep the integrity of the auditlog", so entries outlive the object ([django-auditlog internals](https://django-auditlog.readthedocs.io/en/latest/internals.html)). Envers takes the most normalized approach. It stores "who and why" once per transaction, on a `REVINFO` revision entity, rather than on each audited row ([Envers user guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hibernate/hibernate-orm/main/documentation/src/main/asciidoc/userguide/chapters/envers/Envers.adoc)). That amounts to a command envelope.

Postgres has no option that keeps history in the live row. **PG 18 added temporal constraints (`WITHOUT OVERLAPS`, `PERIOD` foreign keys) but not SQL:2011 system versioning** ([PostgreSQL 18 release notes](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/release-18.html)). The temporal_tables and periods extensions attach a period column to the live row and move old versions into a separate history table ([temporal_tables](https://github.com/arkhipov/temporal_tables); [periods](https://github.com/xocolatl/periods)). supa_audit writes row JSON to one generic `audit.record_version` table, and `old_record` survives the delete ([supa_audit](https://github.com/supabase/supa_audit)). pgaudit writes statements to the server log. It is best-effort and gives "no guarantee that a committed transaction will have a corresponding audit log entry" ([pgaudit](https://github.com/pgaudit/pgaudit)).

The jsonb `metadata` column is a legitimate tool for something other than provenance. The Postgres docs warn that the structure "is typically unenforced" and that "any update acquires a row-level lock on the whole row" ([PG 18 JSON types](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/datatype-json.html)). Heap measured a query taking 584 seconds against about 300 ms with typed columns, because the planner keeps no statistics on jsonb inner keys. Its advice is that values present in most rows should be promoted to columns ([Heap](https://www.heap.io/blog/when-to-avoid-jsonb-in-a-postgresql-schema)). Actor, timestamp and correlation id are present on every row, so they are the strongest candidates for promotion. GitLab requires a JSON schema validator and a 64 KB cap on jsonb, and treats a jsonb column holding "payloads, checkpoints, or serialized state" as a warning sign ([GitLab migration style guide](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/migration_style_guide/)). Stripe's metadata, the canonical sanctioned use, is a capped bag "for your own use" that Stripe itself does not interpret ([Stripe metadata](https://docs.stripe.com/metadata)). That makes it the opposite of an authoritative record. Taken together, the sources sanction jsonb for provenance in two places: as the change payload inside an append-only audit table, and as a bounded bag of non-authoritative annotations.

Event stores and lineage catalogs fill out the picture. KurrentDB, Axon, Marten and Rails Event Store all attach correlation and causation ids, user and headers to **each event**, written in the same append. They then derive provenance views (`$bc-` streams, `correlation-<id>` link streams) as projections ([Kurrent streams](https://docs.kurrent.io/server/v25.1/features/streams.html); [Marten metadata](https://martendb.io/events/metadata.html); [Arkency](https://blog.arkency.com/correlation-id-and-causation-id-in-evented-systems/)). OpenLineage separates Job, Run and Dataset because it "cares how Datasets come into being, not just that relationships exist between them" ([OpenLineage object model](https://openlineage.io/docs/spec/object-model)). Marquez, Apache Atlas and Egeria all treat the producing run or process as an entity in its own right. DataHub is the instructive hybrid. It keeps per-execution truth on separate `DataProcessInstance` entities, and **also projects** an `upstreamLineage` aspect onto the downstream dataset for traversal ([DataHub DataProcessInstance](https://docs.datahub.com/docs/generated/metamodel/entities/dataprocessinstance)). Lineage systems also record deletion as one more provenance fact, such as OpenLineage's `DROP` lifecycle facet or Kurrent's `$streamDeleted` tombstone ([OL LifecycleStateChange](https://openlineage.io/spec/facets/1-0-1/LifecycleStateChangeDatasetFacet.json)). That only works because the provenance lives somewhere other than the deleted object.

| Mechanism | Where provenance lives | Survives deletion of the subject | Records denials |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spring `@LastModifiedBy`, jsonb `metadata` | On the entity row, overwritten | No | No |
| PaperTrail, Audited, simple-history, auditlog | Separate version/audit table | Yes (by default) | No |
| Envers | Shadow table + per-transaction revision entity | ID only by default | No |
| temporal_tables / periods / supa_audit | Period on row, history separate / generic audit table | Yes | No |
| Event stores (Kurrent, Axon, Marten) | Metadata on each event in the aggregate stream | Until scavenged | Only if modeled as a business event |
| Lineage (OpenLineage, Marquez, DataHub) | Run/process entity; DataHub also projects onto dataset | Yes | n/a |
| Cloud audit (CloudTrail, GCP, Azure/Entra) | Provider-owned log plane | Yes | Yes |
| OPA, AuthZed, Auth0 FGA decision logs | PDP-side stream to an external sink | Yes, if routed | Yes, but lossy or short-retained |

## Denials have nowhere to attach, so every serious recorder keeps them apart

A rejected command changes nothing, so there is no state change for provenance to describe. Young's grammar makes the point. A command is imperative, so "the Application Server is allowed to reject" it, while an event is past tense and cannot be un-happened. A rejection is therefore a response, not a negated event on the target ([Young, CQRS Documents](https://cqrs.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/11/cqrs_documents.pdf)). Oskar Dudycz gives the clearest working rule. "Some failures are worth retaining as business data", and should be named as their own events (such as `ProductItemOutOfStock`). Under "selective persistence", "the decision describes the outcome, while the application decides whether it should be durable" ([Dudycz, Throw, Result, or neither?](https://event-driven.io/en/throw-result-or-neither/)). Combined with Evans's filter of recording what "domain experts want to track", this gives the practical test. A denial becomes a durable domain fact when someone accountable cares that it happened. Otherwise it is a result plus logging. There is no canonical consensus beyond that. The 2011 DDD/CQRS mailing-list thread shows practitioners split between synchronous results, failure events and command registries ([dddcqrs thread](https://groups.google.com/g/dddcqrs/c/brjoOs1Z2N0)).

The software makes the structural point concrete. **None of the row-history tools surveyed records a rejected write.** Envers creates a revision "on each commit", and the trigger-based audits fire only on DML that actually executes ([Envers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hibernate/hibernate-orm/main/documentation/src/main/asciidoc/userguide/chapters/envers/Envers.adoc); [Audit trigger 91plus](https://wiki.postgresql.org/wiki/Audit_trigger_91plus)). This is not an oversight. An audit row written in the governed transaction rolls back with it. Recording refusals requires a record committed independently of the effect.

Systems that do record denials keep them in their own plane. AWS CloudTrail records authorization failures in the ordinary event's `errorCode`/`errorMessage` ([CloudTrail record contents](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/awscloudtrail/latest/userguide/cloudtrail-event-reference-record-contents.html)). Google Cloud has a dedicated **Policy Denied** audit-log type ([Cloud Audit Logs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/logging/docs/audit)). Entra logs Conditional Access denials ([Entra PCI-DSS Req. 10](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/standards/pci-requirement-10)). PCI DSS v4.0 10.2.1.4 requires that "audit logs capture all invalid logical access attempts", and 10.2.2 requires a "success and failure indication" on every event, retained 12 months (same source). These records sit apart from the resource and outside the resource owner's write path.

There is a telemetry tier, and it is honest about being one. OPA decision logs do not distinguish allow from deny in the schema. Drop rules can filter them, and "OPA will drop events if the rate limit is exceeded" ([OPA decision logs](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/management-decision-logs)). Auth0 FGA keeps check logs for 7 days ([Auth0 FGA Logging](https://docs.fga.dev/fga-logging)). Open-source SpiceDB has no decision log at all; AuthZed sells a buffered stream to Kinesis or Pub/Sub ([AuthZed audit logging](https://authzed.com/docs/authzed/concepts/audit-logging)). Google bills Policy Denied logs and lets them be excluded, which puts them on the telemetry side of its own split. OWASP says security event logs "should be kept separate" from operational logs, with tamper detection and read-only storage ([OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Logging_Cheat_Sheet.html)).

AI-agent tooling sits squarely in that telemetry tier. MCP says clients SHOULD "log tool usage for audit purposes" but defines no denied-by-policy result type ([MCP tools spec](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/server/tools)). OpenTelemetry's GenAI `execute_tool` span has no approval or denial attribute, so a refused call that never executes may emit no span at all ([OTel execute-tool](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/blob/main/docs/gen-ai/execute-tool.md)). The Claude Agent SDK exposes `PreToolUse` deny decisions and a `PermissionDenied` hook, then advises "If you need to surface hook decisions to your application reliably, log them separately" ([Claude Agent SDK hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/hooks)). The 2026 research goes further. Qin et al. found that local agent harnesses let agents delete their own traces without tripping guardrails, and recommend logging through an interception mechanism outside the agent's control ([arXiv 2609.30266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30266)). aiAuthZ moves the decision off-host and chains "every decision" into a SHA-256 audit log ([arXiv 2607.05518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05518)).

One requirement has almost no precedent: **the decision durably recorded before the effect runs.** CloudTrail delivers in about 5 minutes and "is not guaranteed", Azure in 3 to 20 minutes, and OPA in batches ([How CloudTrail works](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/awscloudtrail/latest/userguide/how-cloudtrail-works.html); [Azure Activity Log](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-monitor/platform/activity-log)). OWASP actively advises that logging failures should not stop the application. Only NIST 800-53 AU-5(4) sanctions shutdown on audit failure, and only as an optional enhancement outside every baseline ([csf.tools AU-5](https://csf.tools/reference/nist-sp-800-53/r5/au/au-5/)). A "no record, no action" design is stricter than mainstream practice. It cannot borrow durability from a telemetry pipeline.

## A hash chain needs a separate structure and an outside anchor

Every tamper-evident design keeps the log as its own append-only structure and publishes a commitment outside the logger's control. Crosby and Wallach treat the logger as untrusted and kept honest by auditors. Their condition is blunt: "a tamper-evident log requires frequent auditing", and without redistributed commitments a logger "is free to have different snapshots make inconsistent claims about the past" ([Crosby & Wallach 2009](https://static.usenix.org/event/sec09/tech/full_papers/crosby.pdf)). Their Merkle history tree turns an 800 MB hash-chain proof over 80 million events into a 3 KB proof. Certificate Transparency signs tree heads and uses consistency proofs between them ([RFC 9162](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9162.html)). Witnesses "MUST persist the new checkpoint before responding" ([C2SP tlog-witness](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/tlog-witness.md)). SQL Server's ledger keeps history in a separate mirrored table and exports digests to immutable storage. Microsoft gives the reason: an attacker who can modify digests could tamper with data and rewrite them ([SQL ledger overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/security/ledger/ledger-overview)). The managed alternative, Amazon QLDB, was retired, and its recommended successor keeps no immutable history by default ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2024/07/aws-kill-qldb), secondary source).

So integrity is a property of a separate structure plus an external anchor. A hash field on a mutable business row shares that row's write path and has no independent tip. A hash chain whose tip is held only by its own store detects alteration of a row, but not truncation or a resealed suffix. That inference follows from the rollback argument in these sources. RFC 9162 does not use the word "truncation".

## A placement rule built from the evidence

No source states the following rule. It is my synthesis of Evans, Young, Fowler, PROV, and the software surveyed above. Classify the fact first, then place it.

| Kind of fact | Example | Place it | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current provenance that the entity's own invariants or UI read | `createdBy`, `lastModifiedAt`, the source a value was imported from | **On the entity**, as typed columns | Spring, Fowler's Temporal Property; cheap reads; lost on delete, which is acceptable here |
| Derivation link: what produced this | "Session state S was produced by tool call C" | **On the result, as a reference** (decision id, run id) | PROV `has_provenance`, DataHub's projected `upstreamLineage`; the intuition holds |
| Opaque, non-authoritative annotations | Client tags, import notes | **On the entity, jsonb**, schema-validated and size-capped | Stripe, GitLab rules |
| A change, with who, when and under what authority | An allowed mutation | **An event in the aggregate's stream**, provenance as typed event metadata | Evans, Young, every event store |
| A decision that precedes, gates or may not produce a result | Authorization verdicts, approvals, **denials** | **A separate append-only decision stream**, keyed by the authority's consistency boundary, written independently of the effect | No result exists to attach to; rollbacks erase same-transaction rows; CloudTrail, GCP, PCI |
| A record that must be trusted against the party it holds accountable | Agent action ledger | **Separate structure with an externally anchored tip** | Crosby & Wallach, CT, SQL ledger, the agent-trace tampering research |
| High-volume diagnostics nobody is held accountable for | Latency, retries, policy-evaluation traces | **Telemetry** (sampled, short retention) | OPA, OTel, OWASP's separation |

The operator's intuition fits the first three rows and half of the fourth. In those rows the thing that needs provenance exists, outlives the question, and is read by the same consumers. It fails in the last three rows, for four distinct reasons, each enough on its own. **Existence**: the record must exist before, or without, the thing. **Cardinality**: one decision or run governs many results. **Lifecycle**: the record must outlive deletion or a rollback of the thing. **Trust**: the record must not share a write path with the party it holds accountable.

## Collapsing the execution-authority ledger would lose denials and pre-effect durability

The current design is in `~/YeeBois/projects/beep-effect/goals/agent-execution-authority/SPEC.md`. It records every MCP dispatch decision "before the effect executes — no record, no action". A denied dispatch produces exactly one row, the decision. Outcome rows bind to their decisions individually. The chain "detects alteration, not truncation", because "nothing anchors the tip". The spec also says a hash "proves correspondence and ordering only". Run the proposal, Session provenance partition plus telemetry for denials, through the rule.

For **allowed dispatches that produce a Session change**, the intuition is right, and the Session should carry that provenance. A reference from the resulting state to the decision that authorized it is exactly PROV's `has_provenance` link and DataHub's projected lineage. It gives the Session a local, cheap answer to "how did I get this way?". If the partition is append-only and holds per-change typed metadata, it is Young's event-metadata pattern and fully canonical. This part of the proposal is an improvement worth making whatever is decided about the ledger.

For **everything else**, the proposal fails at least one test.

**Existence.** A denial produces no governed result. A decision written before the effect precedes its result by definition, and a crash between the two leaves a decision with nothing to attach to. The spec's derived "decided, outcome unknown" state (`verdict = 'allowed' AND outcome IS NULL`) only exists because decisions are rows of their own. If decisions are folded into the result's provenance, that state cannot be expressed. And if the provenance is written in the same transaction as the result, then the effect did not run and the decision was not recorded are the same failure, which is precisely the rollback blindness the audit tools exhibit.

**Cardinality.** Dispatches that touch no Session state, such as reads, outbound publishes, or tools writing under the workspace root, have no Session result to carry their provenance.

**Telemetry for denials.** This moves the most accountability-relevant records into the lossy tier. They become sampled or rate-dropped, short-retained, and absent from the hash chain, and OTel defines no denial attribute to carry them. That reverses the spec's rule "never infer a denial from a caller's error; the policy function records its own typed refusal".

**Architecture.** The packet's boundary says ontology and epistemic never import each other. Putting epistemic decision records into an ontology aggregate either breaks that boundary or makes ontology the owner of authority facts it does not decide.

The steelman for collapsing deserves a fair statement. This is a single-user desktop sidecar. No PCI or NIST regime applies. PGlite connects as table owner, so the chain is only tamper-evident against non-owners. And with no anchored tip, the ledger's integrity advantage over a well-kept audit table is modest today. If nobody accountable will ever ask "what was the agent refused, and when?", Dudycz's selective persistence permits not making denials durable. The cost of a second store is real too: two schemas, a join to reconstruct history, and a separate retention policy.

That argument proves less than it seems, though. The missing anchor is a reason to add one (the `agent-execution-record-anchoring` candidate), not to dissolve the structure that an anchor would protect. And the 2026 trace-tampering evidence is about exactly this case, local harnesses with the agent on the same host.

The middle path costs little and keeps the operator's intuition where it is valid. Treat the ledger as the **Session-keyed decision stream** Young would recognize. The grant set is frozen per session, so the session is the natural consistency boundary and stream key. Keep the stream append-only and independent of the effect, and keep denials in it. Then give the ontology Session's provenance partition a typed reference to the decision id and chain position for every change it records. Telemetry gets a copy of denials for dashboards; it is never the record of them. Under this design, the Session "has its provenance attached" in the PROV sense, while the decision, which must exist first, can exist alone, and must be trusted independently, keeps its own home.

## Conclusion

Asking whether provenance goes on the entity or in a separate store mixes two different facts. **Provenance of a result** answers "where did this come from?" and belongs with the result, at least as a link. **A record of a decision** answers "what was permitted or refused, and on what authority?" and is an entity in its own right. Evans calls it a Domain Event, PROV calls it an Activity, and CloudTrail calls it an event. It cannot be attached to a result that may not exist. The software surveyed is consistent on this, even where the doctrine is silent: nothing that records denials keeps them on the governed object, and nothing that keeps provenance only on the object survives its deletion. For the ledger, the useful question is not whether to collapse it but whether to anchor its tip. Without an anchor, the separate ledger is an honest, ordered, denial-inclusive decision stream. With one, it becomes the tamper-evident record the 2026 agent-governance work says local harnesses lack.

Evidence quality varies. The DDD canon (Evans's Reference, Young, Fowler's 2004–05 drafts) is old and never addresses authorization denials directly. Vernon was unverifiable. The placement rule and the application to the ledger are synthesis. The software and compliance facts above are primary-sourced.

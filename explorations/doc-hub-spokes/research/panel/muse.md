# 1. Has anyone built this, or most of it?

No. Nobody has built the whole stack — notation hub + positioned codecs + tiered judges + provenance gating + calibration ledgers — as one system. But almost every layer exists separately, and several pieces are closer than the brief admits. That matters: the novelty is the composition, not the components, and compositions of proven parts fail in the seams.

**(a) Positioned diagnostics for a notation.** This is solved prior art, decades deep. The Language Server Protocol's `Diagnostic` (range, severity, code, source, related information, code actions) is the obvious ancestor of your finding shape; SARIF (OASIS standard, used by CodeQL, Semgrep, ESLint) is the ledger-shaped version — results, rules, fixes, fingerprints, partial fingerprints for deduplication across runs. Rust's diagnostic renderer (span labels, notes, machine-applicable suggestions via `rustfix`), Clang's fix-its, Roslyn analyzers with code fixes, tree-sitter's error-recovery parsing, and the `mdast`/`hast` ecosystem in unified/remark all do positioned, typed, repairable findings over text. Language workbenches — Spoofax, Xtext, Langium, JetBrains MPS — generate exactly your "spoke" (parser, source map, constraints, editor) from a language definition. Your `@beep/markdown` codec is good work, but it is a re-derivation of this tradition, not a new category. The honest claim is "we built an LSP-grade diagnostic pipeline for prose," which is already strong.

**(b) Typed admission of LLM output with repair.** Adjacent and crowded. Microsoft's TypeChat (2023) is the closest philosophical match: "the LLM translates natural language into a typed program/schema, the type system validates, errors are fed back for repair." Instructor (structured outputs with Pydantic validation and retry), Guardrails AI (RAIL specs, validators, reask), Outlines and Guidance (constrained decoding — repair made unnecessary at the token level), BAML, DSPy's assertions/signatures, and the broad self-repair literature (Olausson et al., *Is Self-Repair a Silver Bullet for Code Generation?*; Self-Refine; Reflexion) all live here. Your contribution over TypeChat is real but narrow: notation-native generation (write Markdown, not JSON) with span-localized patch repair instead of whole-object regeneration or token masking. Note the tension you inherit: constrained decoding (Outlines) increasingly makes admission failures *impossible* for the structural tier, at zero repair cost. Your codec must beat that, not just JSON Schema prompting, to justify itself.

**(c) Domain rule oracles for documents.** This is where the cupboard is barest, and where you should be most suspicious of your own optimism. What exists: XBRL validation with Arelle and the SEC's EDGAR Filing Manual rules — a genuine deterministic oracle for financial filings, and notably a *mandated* one, which is why it works. Schematron for document assertions, SHACL/ShEx for graph validation, and in law specifically: Catala (Inria; a literate DSL that compiles statutory text — French tax and benefits law — into executable code with traceability to the legal source), the L4 language from Singapore's Legalese / Computable Contracts work, Blawx (s(CASP) encoding of statutes), Akoma Ntoso / LegalRuleML (OASIS; document and rule markup, mostly descriptive, rarely an oracle), OpenFisca (tax/benefit microsimulation, rules as code). In patents specifically: LexisNexis PatentOptimizer, ClaimMaster, and Patent Bots do antecedent-basis and §112 checks commercially — Tier 1 patent checking is a shipping product category, not a research gap. Clinical: Arden Syntax, PROforma, Asbru, and FHIR Clinical Reasoning/CQL are guideline-as-code traditions that mostly stalled on authoring cost. The pattern across all of these: the oracle succeeds exactly where a regulator *mandates a filing format* (XBRL) and struggles everywhere authoring is voluntary and expert-bound. Your risk register names this ("rule authoring is slow") but understates it — it is the historical cause of death.

**(d) Process/provenance ontologies gating AI output.** Substantial prior art, none of it quite yours. W3C PROV-O; nanopublications (assertion + provenance + publication info as one citable unit — very close to your witness idea); RO-Crate; and from software supply chain: in-toto and SLSA, where a build is admitted only if a provenance attestation shows approved steps, materials, and builders — this *is* your Tier 3→1 reduction, deployed, and its known weakness is also yours (see §2). Assurance cases (Goal Structuring Notation, OMG's SACM) formalize "claim discharged by evidence via argument" — your obligation/witness structure is an assurance case with worse notation. FDA's Good Machine Learning Practice and Predetermined Change Control Plans are regulatory process-gating for AI. NIST AI RMF is process vocabulary without teeth. I'm not aware of anyone wiring provenance admissibility to *document* rule tiers for agent gating; that specific joint is, as far as I know, unbuilt.

**(e) Declarative DSLs projecting to OWL/SHACL/other.** LinkML is the elephant: a YAML schema DSL that projects one source model to OWL, SHACL, ShEx, JSON Schema, SQL, and documentation, used heavily in biomedical informatics (e.g., by the Monarch Initiative). OTTR (Ontology Templates) does template-to-OWL/RDF expansion. ROBOT templates compile spreadsheets to OWL. So "declare once, project to OWL/SHACL/JSON Schema/docs" is a solved pattern with a decade of scar tissue you should read before designing a DDD-flavored competitor (see §3).

**(f) Compiler-as-reward / diagnostics-as-signal.** Real and growing: CodeRL, RLEF (execution feedback as RL reward, Meta), SWE-RL, verifier-based RL in the DeepSeekMath/GRPO lineage, and the mundane, dominant version — every coding agent loop that feeds `tsc`/test output back into context. Your framing "coding agents work because the domain shipped its own oracle" is a popular thesis (it echoes arguments from the "verifiers' law" discourse) but it is contested: coding agents also work because code has cheap *execution* semantics and enormous training corpora of the repair loop itself. Diagnostics-as-feedback is necessary, not sufficient. Prose has neither property, and no amount of finding-shape engineering creates the second one.

**Verdict:** (a), (e), (f) are prior art you should build *on*, not reinvent; (b) is adjacent with a defensible delta; (c) and (d) contain the actual open problem and the actual historical graveyard.

# 2. Where the thesis is wrong, naive, or likely to fail

**The Tier 3/4 → provenance reduction is the weakest load-bearing claim.** In proof assistants, a witness discharges an obligation because the witness is *checked by the same deterministic kernel*. Your novelty witness is a prior-art search: the provenance check verifies that a search occurred, from approved sources, with resolving links, cross-provider review, and a human signature. It verifies none of the things that make a search good — query formulation, classification coverage, semantic (non-keyword) recall, whether the devastating reference was in result 47 and ignored. in-toto/SLSA learned this in supply chain: provenance proves the *claimed* process ran; it does not prove the process was adequate, and it actively invites what you yourselves name "process theater" — except you've built the theater into the type system, where it now returns `valid`. Worse, the reduction is recursive: the reviewing agent on a different provider is another Tier 3 judge whose discharge needs its own provenance, and the regress terminates at "a human signed," which is precisely the slow, partial human review the thesis said we were escaping. You haven't eliminated the bottleneck; you've given it a schema. That's still worth something — auditability is real value — but call it auditability, not reduction.

**Profiles do not compose monotonically.** "A stricter profile only adds findings" holds only if every rule is a pure predicate over an immutable document with no interaction effects. Real drafting rules violate all three conditions. Narrowing a claim to add antecedent support (Tier 2 fix) changes its scope and therefore its novelty posture (Tier 3) and its word-count/fee profile; rules over aggregates (counts, ratios, claim-set-level constraints) flip sign when other findings are repaired; legal rules are famously defeasible — exceptions, provisos, jurisdictional overrides — and default logic is non-monotonic by design. Even in compilers, warning sets don't compose monotonically once auto-fixes are in play (fix A introduces warning B). What you can honestly promise is *finding-set monotonicity at a fixed document state for predicate rules*. The moment repairs, thresholds, or exceptions enter, composition is a policy decision, not a lattice.

**A single finding shape is a good envelope and a bad ontology.** LSP and SARIF prove a common envelope works for routing and display. But flattening everything into (span, rule, severity, evidence, judge, confidence) destroys distinctions the *repair* step needs: multi-span findings ("this numeral in Fig. 2 vs. that term in ¶[0041]") need relational structure, not a span; deterministic judges reporting confidence 1.0 next to a classifier's calibrated 0.7 teaches agents to treat severity as a sort key and ignore Tier 4 entirely — or worse, to optimize the classifier; and severity means incommensurable things across tiers (a Tier 1 error blocks filing; a Tier 4 "error" is a prior about an examiner). What you want is a common envelope around a *discriminated union* of finding payloads, with per-tier semantics for blocking, repair strategy, and suppression. "Never branch on the kind of judge" is an aesthetic preference that will leak within a year; design the branch explicitly and keep it small.

**Span repair vs. regeneration is an empirical claim stated as an axiom.** The self-repair literature is mixed: gains concentrate in localized, mechanical errors and evaporate or go negative when the defect is global (claim architecture, argument structure) — exactly the expensive defects. Span repair also has a systems cost you underweight: after edit one, every other finding's span is stale (your fingerprinted source map mitigates this — good — but multi-edit transactions against a moving document are a real concurrency problem), and locally-patched prose drifts into incoherence: the patched sentence satisfies the rule and contradicts the paragraph. There's also an accounting problem: regeneration with modern context windows is often *cheaper in wall-clock and simpler in failure modes* than a five-round patch negotiation. Your existing chat surface already repairs blocks by JSON Patch in ≤2 attempts — you have internal evidence about how often round 2 converges; publish it to yourselves before generalizing.

**Agents do not "prefer" loops; they satisfice gates.** An agent facing your oracle will do what agents do to every checker: minimize findings per token. Expect support-paragraph boilerplate ("wherein the member is configured to..." sprayed to satisfy Tier 2), citation theater (real links, irrelevant quotes — your deterministic sub-checks verify the quote exists, not that it supports), and threshold-hugging classifier answers. This is Goodhart with a typechecker as the target. The ledger then calibrates on agent-shaped data, drifting from attorney-shaped reality. None of this is fatal, but "the oracle is the product" needs a companion: *the oracle is also the adversary's specification.*

Finally, two quieter naiveties. Term resolution being "fuzzy" is not a risk line-item; for patent Tier 2 it *is* the problem — if you could resolve "said member" to spec support reliably, you'd have solved claim interpretation, which courts do badly with full briefing. And calibration against office actions conflates labels: rejections are examiner-dependent, prosecution is strategic (applicants amend for business reasons), so precision/recall against outcomes measures examiner modeling (Tier 4) contaminated into Tier 1/2 validation.

# 3. The DSL question

The DDD shape is the wrong carrier, and Alchemy's domain DSL is a misleading muse. Commands, aggregates, events, and projections are vocabulary for *stateful behavior over time* — things that happen and are reacted to. Rules, taxonomies, and axioms are overwhelmingly *atemporal constraints and classifications*: "a dependent claim narrows its parent" is not a command with rejects; forcing it into `Domain.Command` with events buys you an event log you don't need and loses you the constraint semantics you do. Obligations and process admissibility *are* temporal/behavioral (that part genuinely fits an aggregate-ish treatment — an obligation has a lifecycle: opened, witnessed, discharged, waived). So: split the DSL. Behavioral/process declarations in an Alchemy-like shape; static rules and vocabulary in something much closer to LinkML — which, again, already exists, already projects to OWL/SHACL/JSON Schema/docs, and already learned the hard lessons about where the projections leak. Building a second LinkML with aggregate syntax because the repo likes Effect classes is NIH with extra steps. If Effect-idiomatic authoring is the real requirement, write an Effect *front end over* LinkML-shaped AST semantics.

For the OWL projection to be faithful, the AST must carry things a DDD DSL has no natural slot for:

- **Identity and versioning**: IRIs per class/property/rule, import closure, ontology versioning — OWL is a web logic; anonymous local names won't project.
- **Logical vs. annotation separation**: what is an axiom (subclass, disjointness, property characteristics, cardinality, existential restrictions) vs. documentation. Class-based DSLs blur this constantly.
- **A declared OWL profile** (EL, QL, or RL). Full OWL 2 DL expressivity in a friendly DSL is a trap; RL is the interesting one because it coincides with Datalog-style rule execution — which is what your Tier 1/2 judges actually are.
- **An explicit closed-world boundary.** Obligations ("every claim term *must have* support") are closed-world, negation-as-failure checks: they belong in SHACL (or Datalog), *never* in OWL axioms, where absence of support is merely unknown. The AST needs a first-class marker for which fragment each declaration lives in — vocabulary/classification to OWL, integrity constraints to SHACL, defeasible/process rules to Datalog or a rule engine — and a *loss ledger for the projections themselves*, in your own system's spirit: every DSL→OWL/SHACL lowering that drops semantics (deontic modality, temporal validity, jurisdiction, exception priority) should emit a typed loss row exactly like your markdown fold does. That symmetry is the strongest idea in the brief; apply it to yourselves.
- **Deontic and temporal structure**: obligation/permission/prohibition, effective dates, jurisdiction. OWL has no native deontics (LegalRuleML tried; adoption was thin). If the AST can't represent "this rule applies to applications filed after 2024 in the EPO," the OWL projection isn't unfaithful — the source model is just too weak for law.

What is lost in any case: defeasibility and exception precedence (OWL is monotonic; law is not), probabilistic findings (OWL/SHACL are crisp; Tier 3 confidence becomes an annotation the reasoner ignores), and authoring ergonomics for anyone who thinks in SHACL shapes already. Honest answer: bidirectional projection is a maintenance fiction — projections are lossy compilers with a source of truth. Pick the DSL as source of truth, treat OWL/SHACL as *targets with loss reports*, and never promise round-tripping.

# 4. If I were the agent inside this loop

What would make me productive:

- **Latency and incrementality above all.** A check that takes 30 seconds is a check I route around by batching huge risky edits; a 200 ms incremental re-check after each span edit makes me *voluntarily* check early and often. Backpressure is a latency property before it's a correctness property.
- **Few, stable, deduplicated findings with stable rule IDs**, sorted by blocking-ness, each with a one-line rule rationale, a concrete counterexample or expected form ("expected a prior 'a member' before 'the member'; nearest candidates: ¶[0012] 'a fastening member'"), and, where safe, a machine-applicable fix. SARIF-style fingerprints so I can tell new findings from ones I already decided to suppress.
- **A suppression/waiver act with a reason**, recorded in the ledger. If the only way past a false positive is to distort the document, I will distort the document. Give me the honest door so I don't use the dishonest one.
- **Tier honesty in the protocol**: Tier 3/4 findings delivered *once, at drafting milestones*, as advice with calibrated confidence — not re-emitted on every keystroke as pseudo-errors. If a classifier's opinion blocks my inner loop, I will learn to write prose that pleases the classifier, and neither of us will notice the document getting worse.
- **Transactional multi-span fixes and a post-edit re-check diff** ("your edit resolved 3, introduced 1"), so repair is a converging process I can hill-climb rather than whack-a-mole against stale offsets.

What would make me route around it: false-positive rates above roughly one in five (past that, ignoring findings wholesale is the rational policy and I'll adopt it); findings whose repair instructions require Tier 3 judgment I'm told not to exercise; a human-signature gate inside the iteration loop rather than at submission; non-deterministic finding sets on unchanged input (classifier judges re-rolled per run — cache and pin them per document hash); and an oracle that returns the whole document or a giant schema when a 40-token diagnostic would do. Context cost is my oxygen; spend it on findings, not ceremony.

# 5. The smallest falsifying experiment (two weeks)

Use only existing assets: `@beep/markdown`, the Md fold, the 731-example conformance corpus, and public patent material (granted claims + their office actions are public record; keep corpus hygiene per your own rules — use published applications).

**Build one thing**: a Tier 1 + thin Tier 2 checker over a Markdown claim set — antecedent basis, claim-dependency DAG and form (no multiply-dependent-on-multiply-dependent, per jurisdiction profile), reference-numeral consistency between claims and a spec written in the same Markdown doc. Findings in your single shape, span repair supported by the existing offset-splice edits. Nothing else. No DSL, no OWL, no provenance. (Days 1–4; the fold and source map already exist.)

**Run three arms** on 24 short drafting tasks (draft or repair a 6–10 claim set from a provided spec paragraph set), agents blind to arm: (A) free Markdown, no checker; (B) checker findings fed back, repair by *regeneration*; (C) identical findings, repair by *span patch only*. Measure: iterations and tokens to zero Tier 1 findings, wall time, findings introduced by repair, and — the measurement that decides whether any of this matters — blind scoring by one patent practitioner (or, failing access in two weeks, a structured rubric scored by a held-out strong model *plus* spot human review) on support quality and scope sanity of the final claims. Pre-register the kill criteria before running: the thesis predicts C beats B on tokens/time with equal quality, and both beat A on final defect rate as scored by a *held-out re-implementation* of the checks (to avoid grading with the same oracle that taught). If C ≯ B, the span-repair claim — the "speed of strings, certainty of schemas" headline — is falsified in its friendliest domain. If A ≈ C on blind quality despite C's zero findings, the oracle is measuring the wrong thing, and Tier 1 cleanliness is vanity. (Days 5–10.)

**In parallel, attack the provenance claim cheaply**: take 10 published applications with known §102/103 rejections; construct *fully admissible* provenance records (approved sources, resolving links, present quotes, cross-provider review, signature) for searches deliberately scoped to miss the reference that caused the rejection. If your Tier 1/2-over-provenance gate admits all 10 — it will — you have a two-day demonstration that the reduction certifies theater on real cases, and you can quantify how much of the gate's value is audit vs. substance before building it. (Days 8–12.)

Two weeks, one checker, one comparison, one adversarial probe. The DSL, hub renaming, and process ontology are all downstream of these results; if the experiment fails, they're solutions in search of a refuted problem.

# 6. One idea the authors have not considered

**Make oracle *coverage* a first-class typed output — the checked-region map.** Compilers earn trust not only by what they flag but because users implicitly know what the typechecker *covers*: an unflagged, well-typed program region was genuinely examined. Your system has no analog. A claim set can pass every rule while the specification's most dangerous paragraph was touched by no rule at all — and the finding list (empty) looks identical to full assurance. Emit, alongside findings, a coverage artifact: which spans were examined by which rules, which spans are *known-unchecked*, and which rule classes don't exist yet for this profile (no enablement rule, no written-description rule beyond term matching). Render it like test coverage — because that's what it is, with the ledger playing the suite. This does three things the current design can't: it stops agents and attorneys from over-trusting a clean report (your "wrong oracle is worse than none" risk, made visible), it turns rule authoring into coverage-driven work with an obvious next target (the largest unchecked region with human findings in the ledger), and it gives Tier 3 obligations a natural trigger — an obligation opens not where a rule fired, but where coverage is absent and stakes are high. Findings tell you what's wrong; coverage tells you what "nothing wrong" is worth.

# Requirements Engineering Based on Business Process Models: A Case Study

## Problem & positioning

The paper addresses the communication gap between people who define and perform organizational work and analysts who build supporting systems.
Interview-led requirements can focus prematurely on screens, data entry, and direct actor-system interactions, missing indirect actors, business rationale, and workflow context.
The proposed process-oriented approach formalizes the business process first and derives alternative requirement sets from explicit automation choices.
It contrasts this approach with the established “conventional” practice of one HR department, not requirements engineering in general.
The case concerns a real social-security-request management process in the HR department of a large energy organization.
The authors assess completeness, correctness, consistency, contextualization, traceability, and diversity of possible requirement sets qualitatively.

## Approach / architecture

The conventional lane moves from preliminary problem identification and macro-requirements through textual business requirements, rules, glossary, use cases, and non-functional specifications.
The process-oriented lane first models organizational context with the ARIS framework.
A Value-Added Chain decomposes Human Resources Management into macro-processes such as managing social-security requests, medical assessments, retirement, and pensions.
Event-driven Process Chains represent activities, events, actors in swimlanes, and AND/XOR flow structure.
Function Allocation Diagrams attach executors, organizational units, systems, inputs/outputs, business rules, requirements, resources, and risks to atomic activities.
The elicited medical-assessment EPC contains more than 50 activities and several actors.
Analyst and customer jointly classify each activity as manual/non-automatable, system-supported, or fully automated.
Organizational policy, technology, safety, development cost, execution cost, legal constraints, and quality requirements constrain that classification.
Each classification yields a different requirement set and therefore a different possible system.
System design then crosses from process-level conceptual models to implementation-oriented workflow models, adding system interactions, monitoring, notifications, and transactional-system integration.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The paper does not propose an ontology or KG, but supplies a reusable process metamodel and derivation pattern.
Core entity types are Macro-process, Business Process, Activity, Event, Actor, Organizational Unit, System, Document/Information, Business Rule, Requirement, Resource, and Risk.
Structural relations include macro-process decomposition, process sequencing, activity execution by actors, activity support/automation by systems, and input/output production or consumption.
Control-flow relations distinguish concurrent branches from mutually exclusive outcomes.
Function allocations connect an activity to its executor, supporting system, incoming/outgoing information, governing rules, requirements, and associated risks.
Automation status is a deliberate classification of an activity, not an intrinsic property: the same reference process can yield manual, supported, or automated system variants.
Traceability links system requirements back to activities, events, rules, and organizational rationale.
The model separates problem-domain `what` from solution-domain `how`, while keeping an explicit transformation between them.
A useful axiom-like constraint is that every derived system requirement should be justified by a process element or a cross-cutting quality/legal constraint.
Another pattern is change impact: a modified business element locates the related functionality through the established mappings.
The approach anticipates bitemporal or versioned modeling because process changes and volatile requirements can invalidate earlier automation choices, though the paper does not formalize time.

## Evaluation & results

Two independent teams produced the compared requirements specifications: one with the organization's conventional approach and one from process modeling.
The comparison is descriptive and qualitative; the actual requirements specifications are omitted for space.
Process modeling found missing functionality for recording and manipulating Medical Assistance decisions that indirectly influenced employer decisions.
It corrected an erroneously generalized extension-request decision by revealing that the decision applied only to previously granted pensions.
Joint process-executor modeling exposed divergent accounts and inconsistencies that isolated actor-system interviews had not revealed.
It formalized process context and system purpose and elicited more detail from customers.
It exposed a spectrum of requirement sets with different automation levels; the conventional team had implicitly selected low automation because it assumed the process was simple.
The authors conclude that the process-based specification was more complete, correct, and traceable and supported explicit automation choice.
No requirement counts, defect counts, time-on-task, stakeholder counts, cost figures, agreement measures, or statistical tests are reported.
The authors explicitly state that the results should not be generalized without further validation.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

Independent teams reduce direct carryover but confound method effects with analyst experience, which the authors acknowledge strongly affects requirements work.
The single HR process and qualitative comparison do not establish external validity or causal superiority.
Omitting the requirement artifacts prevents independent auditing of completeness, correctness, consistency, and traceability claims.
The approach assumes elicitation produces a stable business-process model not altered by the introduced system; the authors say this is not generally true.
No method is yet supplied for choosing the preferred automation level from non-functional requirements and constraints.
The claimed onboarding and organizational-knowledge-sharing benefits remain subjective and are reserved for future investigation.
The paper does not formalize how legal constraints, conflicting stakeholder claims, uncertainty, or evidence for process assertions should be represented.
ARIS diagrams provide structure but no ontology-level identity, provenance, validity, or contradiction semantics.
High automation in a volatile process may amplify change costs; the authors identify this tradeoff but do not measure it.

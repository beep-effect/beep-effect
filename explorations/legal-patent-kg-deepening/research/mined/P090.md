# UFO-L: A Core Ontology of Legal Concepts Built from a Legal Relations Perspective

## Problem & positioning

The doctoral-consortium paper argues that legal core ontologies often lack direct grounding in primary legal theory, widening the gap between computational representations and legal practice.
Its mapping of 128 studies from 1995–2014 found 35 using primary legal-theory sources, 44 using indirect sources, and 49 using no legal theory as a primary source.
UFO-L is proposed as a legal-core layer over the Unified Foundational Ontology, centered on legal relations rather than the norm-centered perspective attributed to many Kelsen-influenced ontologies.
The legal basis is the structural portion of Alexy’s Theory of Fundamental Rights; weighing, balancing, and dynamic legal argumentation are outside scope.
The intended payoff is reuse, clarity, consistency, completeness, lower modeling cost, and semantic interoperability across legal domains.
This is a research proposal and progress report: it presents a partial taxonomy and validation design, not a finished or empirically validated ontology.

## Approach / architecture

The proposed stack has UFO as the domain-independent foundation and UFO-L as a middle legal-core layer reusable by jurisdiction- or application-specific ontologies.
UFO’s reified `Relator` supplies the principal modeling primitive because a relator existentially depends on multiple individuals and can carry the internal structure of a legal relation.
Alexy supplies the legal distinctions: a norm is the meaning of a normative enunciation; deontological norms divide into rules and principles; principles admit degrees of fulfillment while rules are fulfilled or not.
A legal relation is defined as a bond between subjects achieved by a legal fact, equivalently a social relation typified in a legal norm.
The methodology is iterative and incremental: first model fundamental legal relations, then legal facts, agents, norms, positions, and objects from legal doctrine, legislation, and jurisprudence.
Comparative work against CLO and LRI-Core is intended to test the value of the relation-centered perspective.
Three planned experiments separate novice conceptual modelers, experienced computer professionals, and legal experts interpreting UFO-L models.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The partial taxonomy places `LegalRelator` under `SocialRelator`, which is under UFO `Relator`.
`LegalRelator` specializes into `SimpleLegalRelator` and `ComplexLegalRelator`.
The figure models a complex legal relator as aggregating `2..*` simple legal relators, making compound legal positions explicit rather than flattening them into one edge.
`SimpleLegalRelator` specializes into `RightDutyRelator` and `NoRightPermissionRelator`.
`RightDutyRelator` specializes by action polarity into `RightDutyToAnAct` and `RightDutyToAnOmission`.
`NoRightPermissionRelator` likewise specializes into `NoRightPermissionToAct` and `NoRightPermissionToOmission`.
`ComplexLegalRelator` specializes into `LibertyRelator` and `CompetenceRelator`.
`LibertyRelator` specializes into `ProtectedLiberty` and `UnprotectedLiberty`.
The taxonomy thus reifies correlated legal positions and distinguishes act from omission, simple from compound position structures, liberty protection, and competence.
The paper does not expose the internal subclasses of `CompetenceRelator`; power, liability, immunity, and disability should not be claimed from this source’s visible taxonomy.
Planned additional concepts include legal fact, legal agent or subject, legal norm, legal position, and legal object, but their axioms and relations are not supplied.
The core relation pattern is `legal fact` brings about `legal relator`; the legal relator depends on participating subjects and is typified or regulated by a legal norm.
Compared with legal-act ontologies limited to rights and duties, UFO-L explicitly seeks permissions and no-rights and uses complex relators to bundle several simple correlations.

## Evaluation & results

The systematic mapping sample contains 128 studies: 35 (approximately 27%) used primary legal-theory sources, 44 (approximately 34%) indirect sources, and 49 (approximately 38%) no legal theory as a primary source.
The ontology-use chart reports LRI-Core in 19 legal ontologies, LKIF in 18, DOLCE in 11, CLO in 10, FOLaw in 8, PROTON+OPJK in 8, FBO in 4, and SUMO and NM-Core in 1 each.
Those counts describe the mapped literature and do not evaluate UFO-L.
The completed outputs reported are the systematic mapping publication and earlier UFO-L taxonomy/theoretical work; a newer model and comparative analyses were still in development.
Experiment 1 would compare UML and UFO-L models made by undergraduates with UML/conceptual-modeling knowledge.
Experiment 2 would repeat the scenarios with experienced computer professionals to estimate modeling-background effects.
Experiment 3 would ask legal professionals to interpret UFO-L models and assess correctness, clarity, realism, and communication value.
No participant counts, observed measurements, statistical tests, competency-question results, reasoner checks, or experiment outcomes are reported.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The authors explicitly exclude weighing, balancing, and dynamic legal argumentation, although these are central to Alexy’s account of principles.
The ontology is incomplete in this paper: only a fragment of the legal-relator taxonomy is shown, with no downloadable artifact, namespace, axioms, serialization, or competency questions.
All four experimental hypotheses are prospective, so expected clarity, completeness, interoperability, and reduced cost remain unverified.
The subjectivist relation-centered stance is asserted to better match contemporary practice but is not compared empirically with a norm-centered model here.
The taxonomy conflates paired positions in labels such as `RightDutyRelator`; a KG implementation must still identify each bearer, counterparty, directed content, source norm, and temporal scope.
The relation “typified in a legal norm” lacks explicit semantics for constitutive versus regulative norms, jurisdiction, authority, validity, exceptions, priority, and defeasibility.
Principles’ degrees of satisfaction are described but not modeled, and the promised competence branch is not decomposed in the visible figure.
The 1995–2014 mapping is useful historical evidence but cannot establish current ontology adoption or quality.
P090 and P091 contain essentially the same paper in distinct PDF renderings; they add no independent evidence when synthesized together.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- `semantic-foundation / SKOS and @beep/ontology`: take the layered foundation → legal core → domain ontology architecture and the explicit legal-relator vocabulary; reject treating SKOS broader/narrower links as substitutes for UFO dependence or Hohfeldian correlation axioms.
- `epistemic slice`: model a legal relation as a provenance-bearing entity created by a legal fact and grounded in a source norm; add evidence spans, claim status, contradiction links, and bitemporal validity absent from UFO-L.
- `patent tooling / uspto-patent-driver-depth`: represent ownership, assignment, license, representation, and examiner/applicant procedural positions as legal relators with parties, act/omission content, source record, and effective interval instead of binary edges.
- `patent tooling / agentic-cad-patent-tooling`: use protected/unprotected liberty and competence as candidate review dimensions for proposed actions, but require a deeper Hohfeldian model before automating authorization conclusions.
- `OIP solo-practice workflows`: take the complex-relator aggregation pattern for matters that bundle duties, permissions, liberties, and competences; surface every simple constituent and source document for attorney review.
- `agent memory / Cognee`: preserve legal facts, norms, positions, and relators as different node kinds so retrieval does not collapse a source statement, its interpretation, and the legal relation asserted from it.

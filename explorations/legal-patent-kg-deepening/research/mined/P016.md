# AgentODRL: A Large Language Model-Based Multi-Agent System for ODRL Generation

## Problem & positioning

- ODRL enables machine-readable data-rights policies, but authoring requires RDF, serialization, and ODRL conceptual-model expertise that many legal and domain experts lack.
- Translating natural-language regulations or agreements into ODRL must satisfy strict syntax while preserving parties, assets, actions, permissions, prohibitions, duties, and constraints.
- Monolithic LLM generation performs poorly when it must simultaneously resolve legal dependencies, segment parallel policies, and produce constrained graph syntax.
- High-quality natural-language-to-ODRL corpora are scarce, and earlier approaches mostly cover structurally simple policies.
- AgentODRL positions task-specialized agents and dynamically selected workflows as the remedy for parallel and recursive legal-policy structures.

## Approach / architecture

- A central Orchestrator classifies input complexity and chooses a path through three Workers: Rewriter, Splitter, and Generator.
- Simple path: Orchestrator → Generator.
- Parallel path: Orchestrator → Splitter → policy units → Generator.
- Recursive path: Orchestrator → Rewriter → Splitter → policy units → Generator.
- The Rewriter performs structure-preserving inlining: it resolves explicit clause-number references and implicit dependencies while retaining clause separation.
- The Splitter starts a new unit only when core asset, assigner-assignee relation, or fundamental policy purpose changes, then assigns Agreement, Offer, or Set.
- The Generator uses an ODRL ontology-guided prompt plus two corrective mechanisms.
- Semantic reflection pairs the generator with a LoRA-finetuned Qwen3-4B-Instruct expert that extracts a semantic-checkpoint list for output comparison.
- Syntactic correction runs a generate-validate-correct loop using PYSHACL against SHACL constraints derived from the ODRL information model and vocabulary.
- Validation errors are fed back until the output passes or reaches a configured reflection limit.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- The policy-information tuple is `Rinfo = (P, A, Ac, Cpolicy, Ccontext)`.
- `P` covers `Party` roles, specifically assigner and assignee; `A` is the target `Asset`; `Ac` is the permitted or regulated `Action`.
- `Cpolicy` contains core clauses typed as `Permission`, `Prohibition`, or `Duty`; `Ccontext` contains contextual constraints.
- Natural-language cases are classified as `Simple`, `Parallel`, or `Recursive` according to semantic structure rather than length alone.
- Recursive dependency is modeled as one clause's enforcement depending on another clause; preprocessing creates self-contained units without discarding the original clause structure.
- Parallel policy segmentation preserves distinct party/asset/purpose combinations rather than forcing them into one ODRL graph.
- SHACL constraints operationalize structural conformance to the ODRL vocabulary and information model.
- Semantic checkpoints form a second representation of indivisible source meaning, used to compare the generated graph against the natural-language policy.
- The architecture separates routing, dependency resolution, semantic segmentation, graph generation, syntactic validation, and semantic checking into auditable stages.
- The paper does not define provenance links from ODRL nodes or checkpoints back to exact source spans.

## Evaluation & results

- The benchmark has 770 manually reviewed cases: 440 simple, 220 parallel, and 110 sequential-dependency/recursive cases.
- It begins with 70 seeds—40 simple, 20 parallel, and 10 sequential-dependency—then uses Gemini 2.5 Pro for augmentation under logic-preservation and context-transformation rules.
- The semantic validator was LoRA-tuned on 2,380 synthetic samples with rank 16, alpha 32, one NVIDIA 4090, and 3 epochs; reported train loss is 0.129 and validation loss 0.0668.
- Experiment 1 uses GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, and GPT-4.1-nano; every configuration runs three times and averages results, with at most 8 reflections.
- Against SCR-Enhanced across all models, the full pipeline improves average grammar score by 5.39% and semantic score by 14.52%.
- Full-pipeline all-case results are 99.89 grammar / 97.93 semantic for GPT-4.1; 99.36 / 96.43 for mini; and 92.01 / 72.35 for nano.
- On nano recursive cases, semantic score rises from 34.87 under ontology guidance to 61.53 under the full pipeline, reported as a 76.46% improvement.
- In the workflow study, Splitter → Generator raises parallel-case semantic score from 69.44 to 84.88; Rewriter → Splitter → Generator reaches 82.00 on recursive cases.
- Automated orchestration scores 80.22 semantic overall versus 72.35 for Generator only, but trails fixed Splitter and full paths at 84.02 and 88.07.
- Orchestration consumes about 46.2 million tokens, versus 47.9 million for forced Splitter and 49.5 million for the forced full path; Generator only uses about 33.9 million.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- The paper has no dedicated limitations section and makes few explicit negative findings.
- The benchmark is mostly synthetic: 700 of 770 cases result from LLM augmentation of 70 seeds, which may preserve template regularities despite manual review.
- The semantic ground truth is extracted by an Identifier LLM and judged by two LLM jurors, not independently annotated legal-expert mappings; correlated model bias can inflate semantic scores.
- Grammar score normalizes SHACL violation counts, so a high percentage is not necessarily equivalent to complete conformance, executability, or legal validity.
- Evaluation covers only the GPT-4.1 model family for generation and does not report cross-provider routing robustness.
- The orchestrator does not reach the best manually selected workflow and sometimes reduces grammar score while improving semantics.
- Token costs are very high, and the comparison does not report latency or monetary cost.
- Clause inlining can erase the significance of citation, amendment, exception scope, or versioned incorporation unless provenance and temporal identity are preserved separately.
- The system evaluates policy translation, not enforcement behavior, adversarial clauses, jurisdictional interpretation, or lawyer acceptance.
- The source is a first-version arXiv manuscript dated November 2025 in the extracted text; peer-review status is not established by the PDF.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- `goals/agentic-cad-patent-tooling`: take complexity-sensitive routing and specialized rewrite/split/generate workers, but require typed stage outputs, deterministic validators, bounded retries, and preserved source-span lineage.
- OIP solo-practice workflows: use a lawyer-facing natural-language-to-policy draft flow with side-by-side semantic checkpoints and SHACL errors; approval, not the agent score, should make a policy operational.
- `goals/epistemic-bitemporal-edge-core`: preserve every clause reference, inlining decision, generated policy version, validator report, and reviewer action as additive evidence with valid/known times.
- `goals/epistemic-contradiction-triage`: adapt checkpoint lists to compare permissions, prohibitions, duties, parties, assets, and constraints; retain conflicts as candidates rather than letting the Rewriter silently harmonize them.
- `goals/semantic-foundation`: reuse ODRL vocabulary and SHACL validation at the policy boundary, but reject projecting ODRL's operational rule graph directly into the SKOS concept-scheme core.
- Agent memory (Cognee): take specialized worker memory and compact checkpoints as scoped context, while keeping synthetic cases and prior outputs clearly separate from authoritative legal evidence.

# A Systematic Mapping of the Literature on Legal Core Ontologies

## Problem & positioning

- Legal-ontology research had expanded across topics and approaches, but the narrower legal core ontology (LCO) field lacked an up-to-date map.
- The authors distinguish LCOs—generic legal concepts, properties, and relations—from legal domain, task, and application ontologies.
- Their motivating concern is that many purported LCOs rely on positivist legal theories, indirect theoretical sources, or no foundational ontology.
- The study is deliberately broad and horizontal: it maps publication volume and contribution types rather than synthesizing effect sizes.
- It is also preparatory work for a unified legal core ontology grounded jointly in legal theory and a foundational ontology.
- Three research questions cover existing LCO work and niches, legal theories used, and foundational/core ontologies used.

## Approach / architecture

- The systematic mapping follows a five-stage flow: planning, search, screening, keywording/classification, and data extraction.
- The universe is legal ontologies; the sample is English-language Computer Science work on legal core ontologies from 1995–2014.
- Included papers and book chapters concern Computer and Law and refer to generic legal concepts, legal theory, or Hohfeldian concepts.
- Abstract-only items, slides, technical reports, duplicates/older versions, non-English work, and purely legal/philosophical ontology work are excluded.
- Google Scholar is the sole search source, spanning venues and publishers indexed there.
- A control group is used to tune a compound search string combining LCO terminology with Hohfeld, legal-theory, or legal-concept terms.
- Abstracts, keywords, introductions, and references are inspected for classification.
- Data extraction classifies contribution as language, method, tool, or model, then records use of legal theory and foundational ontology.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- The map supplies a useful ontology-of-ontologies distinction: `LegalCoreOntology`, `LegalDomainOntology`, `LegalTaskOntology`, and `LegalApplicationOntology` should not be conflated.
- Contribution types form another controlled facet: language, method, tool, and model.
- The catalogued LCO family includes FOLaw, Kralingen's ontology, CLO, NM-L/NM-core, LRI-Core, Legal-RDF, PROTON+OPJK, OPLK, LKIF-core, LegalRuleML-core, and LOTED core.
- Legal-theory provenance is modeled implicitly in three evidence classes: direct primary source, indirect source through an LCO, or no primary source.
- Named theory families include Legal Positivism, Inclusive Positivism, Legal Realism, Legal Interpretivism, and Legal Argumentation.
- Foundational/core grounding candidates reported include LKIF, LKIF-core, LRI-CORE, SUMO, DOLCE, CLO, FOLAW, and OPJK.
- The authors' analysis warns that labels are unreliable: many works called “legal ontology” or appearing generic are actually law-fragment domain ontologies.
- Hohfeld's claim/duty and related classifications are prominent, but the authors argue that positivist foundations omit modern concepts tied to explicit social reality.
- The map therefore supports provenance edges such as `ontology groundedIn theory`, `study usesPrimarySource`, and `artifact hasContributionType`, with source-quality distinctions retained.

## Evaluation & results

- The search returns 269 studies; screening leaves 128 selected studies.
- Publication counts shown for 1995–1999, 2000–2004, 2005–2009, and 2010–2014 are 3, 21, 56, and 50 respectively.
- Those four chart values total 130 rather than the stated 128 selected studies; the paper does not explain the discrepancy.
- Excluding review and opinion papers yields 115 contribution-classified studies: 3 language, 17 method, 36 tool, and 59 model.
- Models are therefore the largest contribution category; language is the smallest.
- Of 128 selected works, 35 (approximately 27%) use primary legal-theory sources, 44 (approximately 34%) use indirect sources, and 49 (approximately 38%) use no primary source.
- The authors identify 47 studies proposing a kind of ontology; 32% are not grounded in a foundational/core ontology.
- Exactly 16 studies directly refer to Hohfeld's classification, with additional studies referring to it indirectly.
- Legal Positivism is the most frequently cited doctrine; the paper provides a chart but no exact doctrine-by-doctrine counts in text.
- Most proposed ontologies in the sample are judged to be domain ontologies, leaving relatively few genuine LCOs.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- Authors' own: omitting the broad phrases “legal ontology/ontologies” from the search string may miss LCO work using only those labels.
- Authors' own: works using synonyms such as “concept of law,” without the selected theory/concept terms, may also be missed.
- Authors' own: the model-heavy result is partly induced by a search designed to find LCO proposals; other strings are needed for languages, tools, methods, and applications.
- The 1995–2014 boundary makes the map historical and unsuitable as a current inventory without an update.
- Google Scholar alone limits reproducibility: the paper does not report a frozen result set, per-query counts, or independent database replication.
- The paper does not report dual-screening agreement, extraction agreement, quality appraisal, or risk-of-bias scoring for included studies.
- Several numeric families have denominator subtleties, and the publication chart conflicts with the stated selected-study total.
- Grounding in a named foundational ontology is treated as a quality signal, but actual axiom quality, interoperability, maintenance, and task fitness are not evaluated.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- **semantic-foundation (SKOS/@beep/ontology):** preserve the core/domain/task/application distinction and attach explicit legal-theory plus foundational-ontology provenance to every imported scheme; reject “legal ontology” as sufficient scope metadata.
- **epistemic slice (claims and evidence spans):** encode direct-primary, indirect, and absent theoretical support as separate evidence relations so a reused ontology cannot inherit authority merely through citation chains.
- **epistemic contradiction triage:** retain the paper's conflicting totals and competing ontology classifications as reviewable claims with denominators and source spans rather than normalizing them away.
- **patent tooling / uspto-patent-driver-depth:** classify patent vocabularies separately from generic legal-core concepts and record whether each construct is a document schema, task model, domain ontology, or genuine reusable core.
- **OIP solo-practice workflows:** favor task- and application-ontology layers for concrete attorney workflows, mapped to—but not collapsed into—the legal core; the map shows that “core” scope claims need scrutiny.
- **agent memory (Cognee):** store literature-map tags for recall and discovery, while keeping study selection, counts, and theory-grounding judgments as provenance-bound deterministic records.

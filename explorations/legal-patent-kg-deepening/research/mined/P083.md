# The Law as a Dynamic Interconnected System of States of Affairs

## Problem & positioning

- Legal knowledge representation needs an explicit account of both change and rule-mediated dependence: regulations change, acts occur, rights arise, and one legal fact can constitute another.
- The authors propose a highly abstract legal ontology intended as a source of modeling heuristics rather than a domain-specific expert system or complete reasoning calculus.
- Its three primitives are states of affairs, events, and rules; together they model law as a dynamic, interconnected system.
- The central distinction is between causation, which changes what obtains over time, and constitution, through which one obtaining state of affairs supports another timelessly.
- The paper positions the model as a legal top ontology that can be refined by more detailed accounts of norms, acts, concepts, defeasible reasons, and legal functions.

## Approach / architecture

- The paper first defines its primitives and their connections, then analyzes sales contracts, classification, rights, proof, juristic acts, validity, and juristic facts.
- A final formalism reifies states of affairs, events, and rules as individuals so sentences can be about event occurrence and rule validity.
- Events have finite sets of initial and final states of affairs; causal rules connect an event's occurrence plus its initial conditions to its final states.
- Rules have one or more generic condition states and one conclusion state; their instances connect concrete states of affairs.
- Legal effects can occur on several levels: signing a contract causes a signed document and, through supervening acts and constitutive rules, a bond, obligation, and duty.
- The framework separates rules in the strict sense, principles, and goals while retaining all three under a broad rule primitive.
- Seven elicitation heuristics guide a modeler through primitives, supervenience, event transitions, rule effects, rule kind, defeasibility and exceptions, and proof viewpoints.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- `StateOfAffairs` is a possible world-part expressed by a sentence; an obtaining state is a fact, while a non-obtaining one remains representable for hypotheses and rules.
- States are typed as temporary or durable; temporary present states can begin or end, while momentary states obtain only for a moment.
- Modal states divide into anankastic, deontic, and probabilistic families; legal competence is primarily anankastic capability, not merely permission.
- `PointOfView` scopes states of affairs, allowing civil-law and criminal-law findings to conflict without making the entire representation inconsistent.
- `Event` changes obtaining states and is also representable through the state of affairs that the event occurs; acts and juristic acts specialize events.
- `Rule` is a directed connection from condition states to one conclusion state; rule existence/validity is itself a state of affairs and can be the conclusion of another rule.
- Constitutive rules ground supervenience; causal rules connect event occurrence and context to changed states.
- Weak rules are defeasible through undercutters/exceptions or incompatible conclusions resolved by priority or reason-weighing; strong rules guarantee conclusions.
- Principles generate reasons rather than direct conclusions; goals are modeled as underdetermined half-rules that can support multiple means.
- Classification includes subsumption and imputation; an exception is another state of affairs that blocks a rule connection rather than erasing the classified facts.
- Rights are legal states with institutive/acquisition events, consequential rules, and terminative/loss events; the same pattern covers claims, property rights, and human rights.
- Proof is viewpoint-relative: evidence states plus a rule of proof constitute a `Proven(S)` state, which can feed downstream liability rules.
- Juristic acts supervene on brute acts plus competence conditions; valid act, valid product, and valid rule form distinct, chained states.
- The formal vocabulary includes `STATES_OF_AFFAIRS`, `EVENTS`, `RULES`, `OCCURS`, `EXISTS`, event initial/final mappings, rule conditions/conclusion, and `RULES_OF_EVENT`.
- States, occurrences, and validities are reified but not conflated: an event differs from its occurrence, and a rule differs from the state that it exists.

## Evaluation & results

- The evaluation is conceptual, consisting of worked analyses and a formal appendix; no corpus experiment, reasoner benchmark, annotation study, or quantitative result is reported.
- Seven legal topics are reconstructed in the same vocabulary: contract formation, classification, rights, proof, juristic acts, validity, and juristic facts.
- The rights analysis unifies otherwise different claims, property rights, and human rights as states whose creation, consequences, and termination are rule-governed.
- The validity analysis distinguishes three layers: validity of a legislative act, its resulting legal product, and the rules created through that product.
- The proof example explains how contrary civil and criminal findings can coexist by attaching each proven state to its evidentiary standard and legal viewpoint.
- Comparison with McCarty, Valente, Van Kralingen/Visser, and Reason-Based Logic suggests those systems refine portions of this deliberately minimal ontology.
- The formal appendix demonstrates translations from the paper's diagrams into set-theoretic mappings and partial-world representations.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- The authors explicitly describe the model as highly abstract and invite refinements; it does not encode detailed internal structures of norms, acts, concepts, or legal arguments.
- The formal appendix explicitly leaves defeasibility out and directs readers to other work, despite defeasibility being central to the prose model.
- Rules formally use concrete states rather than generic states; the authors acknowledge that variables and instantiation are needed for realistic rules.
- States of affairs receive implicit timestamps, which the authors note weakens their exact correspondence to sentences.
- Causation is represented as rule-governed transition, but transaction time, valid time, retroactivity, delayed effect, and uncertainty are not modeled.
- `PointOfView` prevents global inconsistency but lacks authority, jurisdiction, court, burden, standard of proof, and precedence semantics.
- The broad `Rule` primitive covers physical laws, proof rules, norms, principles, and goals, creating a practical risk of category collapse without explicit subtypes.
- The model says exceptions block links but does not formalize exception provenance, priority, scope, reinstatement, or contradiction-resolution outcomes.
- Its language-relative state-of-affairs identity needs a robust canonicalization policy before two textual claims can safely be treated as the same KG node.
- There is no empirical evidence that the heuristics improve modeling quality, interoperability, or legal retrieval.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- **semantic-foundation (SKOS/@beep/ontology):** Adopt the small named core—state of affairs, event, rule, occurrence, validity—and specialize it; reject a single undifferentiated edge for causal transition and constitutive support.
- **epistemic slice:** Turn point-of-view proof into scoped claims carrying legal forum, proof standard, evidence spans, valid time, transaction time, and explicit undercutter/priority edges.
- **patent tooling (uspto-patent-driver-depth):** Model filing, publication, examination, allowance, abandonment, and assignment as events whose effects are separate from constitutive status rules and rule-validity assertions.
- **OIP solo-practice workflows:** Reuse the institutive/consequential/terminative pattern for matters, engagements, duties, permissions, and deadlines, preserving why each state began and ended.
- **agent memory (Cognee):** Store event, event-occurrence claim, rule, rule-validity claim, and derived state as distinct nodes so remembered conclusions remain auditable and temporally replayable.

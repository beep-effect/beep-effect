# Accelerating Knowledge Graph and Ontology Engineering with Large Language Models

## Problem & positioning

- Knowledge graph and ontology engineering tasks—modeling, modification, population, alignment, and entity disambiguation—remain labor-intensive and difficult to automate at useful quality and scale.
- LLMs can act as approximate natural-language knowledge bases and structured-output assistants, but hallucination, reasoning limits, prompt size, and open-ended tasks prevent dependable one-shot automation.
- The paper frames LLM-based KGOE as an emerging research area and argues that conceptual modularity is central to making hard tasks tractable.
- Its intended operating mode is semi-automatic or human-LLM interactive: LLMs produce drafts or bounded operations, while domain and ontology experts check factual and semantic accuracy.
- This is primarily a position and research-agenda paper, supported by results from cited prior work rather than a new controlled experiment performed in the paper.

## Approach / architecture

- Apply divide-and-conquer to both human and LLM cognition by partitioning large ontologies into coherent conceptual modules.
- A module contains the classes, properties, and axioms relevant to a domain-expert-recognized key notion for a use case.
- Modules are human-centric rather than mechanically exact partitions: they can overlap or nest and must make sense from domain, use-case, and available-data perspectives.
- The Modular Ontology Modeling (MOMo) approach combines a stepwise design method, ontology design patterns, the OWL-based OPLa module-description language, design-pattern libraries, and tooling.
- OPLa annotations make module boundaries programmatically identifiable, allowing prompts and KG operations to select only relevant schema slices.
- An ABox inherits modular structure from its ontology: the graph module contains ABox statements fully within the associated ontology module.
- For complex alignment, the demonstrated prompt sequence first selects relevant named modules from a list, then generates the alignment-rule body from those modules.
- For modeling, LLMs can draft reusable ontology design patterns and then instantiate and connect them rather than attempting an entire ontology in one shot.
- For population, provide a compact module schema plus an extraction example, constraining output to a consistent vocabulary and scope.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- `ConceptualModule` is an explicit ontology object grouping relevant `Class`, `ObjectProperty`, `DataProperty`, and `Axiom` resources around a key notion.
- `ModuleMembership` is many-to-many: ontology elements may appear in overlapping modules, and modules may nest.
- Module boundaries are use-case-sensitive design assertions, not universal logical truths or necessarily disjoint partitions.
- OPLa supplies machine-readable module and pattern descriptions inside OWL, enabling discovery, prompt construction, documentation, and maintenance.
- Ontology design patterns provide small, reusable semantic units; simple “micropatterns” can seed a programmatically accessible design library.
- Modular ABoxes mirror schema modules, giving population and retrieval a bounded graph context.
- Named modules such as `Organization`, `PhysicalSample`, `Trajectory`, `Cruise`, `Event`, `Place`, and `Provenance` bridge expert concepts and data reality.
- Complex alignment is modeled as rules, not only one-to-one class equivalence; relevant modules narrow the search space for rule-body atoms.
- The architecture treats schema diagrams and module documentation as operational inputs to LLM workflows, not merely human-facing prose.
- Modularity is proposed from the start of ontology design so later extension, alignment, population, and disambiguation share the same boundaries.

## Evaluation & results

- Scale motivation includes ENVO with over 2,100 classes, Gene Ontology with over 40,000, a Wikipedia-derived hierarchy with over 2 million, DBpedia Core with about 900 million triples, and KnowWhereGraph with over 28 billion triples.
- The GeoLink alignment benchmark includes one ontology with 40 classes, 149 object properties, and 49 data properties; the other has 156, 124, and 46 respectively.
- On 109 complex alignment rules, module information enabled at least one correct body atom for 104 rules, compared with 5 without module information.
- All body atoms were found for 45% of rules, and 46% of rules had no incorrect body atom in system output.
- The paper summarizes another complex-alignment approach at semantic F1 scores of 0.49–0.69, depending on the ontology pair.
- A cited modular population experiment reports approximately 90% extraction of related triples from text against ground truth.
- Entity-disambiguation benchmarks are said to reach F1 up to 91 after prompt tuning, but the authors warn that benchmark leakage makes current capability hard to assess.
- The paper reports no new dataset or direct experiment of its own and states that no data was used for the article.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- Authors' own: some evidence for modularity's benefits is qualitative, and broader systematic experiments are still needed.
- Authors' own: current LLMs lack sufficient commonsense and reasoning power for fully automated ontology learning.
- Authors' own: existing alignment benchmarks may be under-specified, human-disputed, overly simple, or contaminated by training data.
- Authors' own: the effect of other logical or KG modularization definitions on LLM performance is not known.
- Authors' own: more real-world, non-synthetic benchmarks derived after application-driven curation are needed.
- MOMo module boundaries intentionally lack precise membership rules; this aids expert fit but creates reproducibility and governance questions.
- Reported alignment and population gains come from cited studies with different setups, so they do not constitute one comparable benchmark suite.
- “Approximately 90%” extraction does not state precision/recall decomposition here, and extracted triples can still be semantically or evidentially wrong.
- Modules reduce context but can hide cross-module constraints; overlapping-module selection and global consistency require explicit checks.
- Human review is assumed but not operationalized with approval records, provenance, competency questions, or cost measurements.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- `goals/semantic-foundation`: take explicit, named, overlapping conceptual modules around SKOS schemes and `@beep/ontology` patterns; keep module membership separately governed from SKOS broader/narrower semantics.
- `goals/epistemic-bitemporal-edge-core`: store module assignment as a sourced, revisable projection with valid/known times rather than mutating a single timeless partition; retain cross-module evidence edges.
- `goals/epistemic-contradiction-triage`: route extraction and comparison through the smallest relevant module set, then run a cross-module constraint pass so divide-and-conquer does not suppress contradictions at boundaries.
- `explorations/uspto-patent-driver-depth` and `goals/agentic-cad-patent-tooling`: decompose patent KGs into claim, party, citation, prosecution-event, technical-function, and evidence modules before asking agents to align or populate them.
- OIP solo-practice workflows: expose module-scoped draft/review queues with competency questions and evidence spans, preserving the paper's human-expert check as a durable approval event.
- Agent memory (Cognee): use conceptual module names as retrieval filters and context-budget boundaries, but reject treating operator-memory clusters as authoritative ontology membership.

# Legal Ontology and the Problem of Normativity

## Problem & positioning

Applied ontology needs a category system for legal and socio-political institutions that accounts for more than one kind of normativity.
The authors argue that Hart, Rawls, and Searle each illuminate institution-generated normativity but treat it as if it exhausted the field.
That omission makes an ontology incomplete when it models what institutional rules permit or require but cannot represent moral criticism of the institution itself.
The paper positions normativity as ontologically plural: facts about rules, moral obligations, and evaluative structures of intentional states are not interchangeable.
Its contribution is diagnostic and philosophical, not an implemented legal ontology.

## Approach / architecture

The paper compares three rule distinctions across analytic philosophy.
Hart distinguishes primary duty-imposing rules from secondary power-conferring rules that create, change, interpret, or enforce rules.
Hart’s “soft positivism” adds an internal acceptance of legal rules: a traffic signal is not merely predictive but supplies a reason to stop.
Rawls distinguishes inductive summary rules from logically prior practice rules that make institution-specific actions possible.
Searle distinguishes regulative rules governing antecedently existing behavior from constitutive rules that create or define new behavior.
All three use games as the paradigm: rule-governed institutional acts acquire an `ought` from the constitutive system.
The authors contrast this logical or “soft” normativity with obligations such as respecting persons, which need not arise from a promise or institution.
They then add a third family grounded in the immanent structure of intentional phenomena, including blame, praise, belief, intention, and apology.
The conclusion proposes that a sound ontology must keep these three sources of normative force distinct while relating them to acts, institutions, and obligations.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The first proposed category is constitutive-rule normativity: obligations and powers that exist because an institution’s rules create the relevant act or status.
Associated entities include `Rule`, `Primary Rule`, `Secondary Rule`, `Practice Rule`, `Constitutive Rule`, `Institution`, `Speech Act`, and `Institutional Obligation`.
Relations include rule-creates-institution, institution-enables-act, speech-act-creates-obligation, rule-confers-power, and rule-imposes-duty.
The second category is robust moral/natural-law normativity, whose obligations are not reducible to institution membership or agreement.
It permits claims such as a law being morally wrong, a promise failing to obligate because its object is immoral, or human rights overriding sovereignty.
The third category is intentional/evaluative normativity arising from mental phenomena: intentional harm can be more blameworthy than accidental harm, and awareness of harm can imply apology.
Associated entities include `Intentional State`, `Belief`, `Desire`, `Intention`, `Evaluation`, `Blame`, `Praise`, and `Moral Obligation`.
The paper implies a priority relation: intentional capacities are biologically and ontologically prior to speech acts, even when speech-act vocabulary is used to explain them.
It also implies an override relation between normative sources—for example, moral considerations can defeat an institutionally generated promissory obligation.
Crucially, speech acts, institutions, and obligations should exist as related categories rather than obligations being analyzed away as logical consequences.
For KG use, the analysis argues against a single generic `obligates` edge; provenance of normative force and possible override conditions must be explicit.
No formal axioms, consistency conditions, serialization, or executable reasoning semantics are supplied.

## Evaluation & results

The evaluation is a comparative philosophical argument, not an empirical study.
Hart is tested against the inability of command theory to distinguish being coercively obliged from having a de jure obligation.
Rawls is tested through the difference between rules summarized from past conduct and rules constituting practices such as chess, promising, and punishment.
Searle is tested against the claim that deriving “ought” from “is” in promising establishes only an institution-relative, largely definitional obligation.
Counterexamples include immoral promises, morally criticizable legal institutions, human-rights limits on sovereignty, and equity overriding formal justice.
The authors conclude that legal/social ontology needs at least three kinds of normativity.
No dataset, ontology implementation, user study, expert panel, quantitative metric, or competency-question suite is reported.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The authors explicitly state that they do not present a systematic framework for the variegated forms of normativity.
They leave the truth of moral realism open and say a sound ontology would require a fuller discussion of it.
The three-family classification is argued by examples but not supplied with necessary and sufficient membership criteria.
“Logical,” “moral,” and “intentional” normativity can interact or overlap; the paper does not formalize conflict detection or priority among them.
The critique focuses on selected readings of Hart, Rawls, and Searle and does not survey deontic logic, defeasible norms, Hohfeldian relations, or later legal-ontology implementations.
The model handles neither legal validity over time nor the institutional sources, jurisdictions, authorities, and procedures that determine positive-law status.
Robust moral claims are difficult to encode without confusing asserted moral belief, community standard, judicial holding, and ontology-level truth.
An implementation should therefore model moral propositions as attributed claims unless stronger commitments are explicitly chosen.
The paper’s use of “at least three” appropriately leaves the taxonomy open but limits its direct completeness as an engineering specification.

# Towards a Legal Core Ontology Based on Alexy's Theory of Fundamental Rights

## Problem & positioning

The paper argues that legal core ontologies cannot be theory-neutral because competing legal theories answer “what is law?” differently.
An ontology based on Kelsen, Cossio, or Alexy will expose different concepts, and downstream legal-domain ontologies and applications inherit those commitments.
The authors criticize both legal-positivist overrepresentation and legal ontologies that cite no primary legal theory.
They select Alexy's Theory of Fundamental Rights because it distinguishes rules from principles and develops legal relations through Hohfeldian correlatives and opposites.
The target, UFO-L, is a legal-core layer over the Unified Foundational Ontology rather than a standalone vocabulary.
This initial paper narrows Alexy to rights to something: rights to negative acts, rights to positive acts, and their corresponding permissions and no-rights.
Liberties, competences, balancing, and weighing are explicitly deferred.
The desired downstream uses are legal-domain ontologies, interoperable knowledge bases, languages, frameworks, and applications.

## Approach / architecture

The work combines documentary legal analysis with foundational-ontology specialization.
Alexy supplies the legal notions of norm, legal position, right, duty, permission, and no-right; Hohfeld supplies correlation and opposition.
UFO supplies identity, parthood, dependence, agents, roles, normative descriptions, events, social moments, and especially relators.
UFO-A models endurants and relational moments, UFO-B events, and UFO-C agents, social roles, social objects, and normative descriptions.
OntoUML carries the UFO distinctions into the conceptual model.
The main modeling move is to shift the center from the norm to the legal relation and reify that relation as a `Legal Relator`.
Legal agents contingently instantiate legal roles when bound by the relator; paired legal moments constitute the legal relation.
The proposed research program is iterative: begin with fundamental legal relations, then extend toward legal facts, agents, norms, and objects.
The authors propose eventual comparison with CLO and LRI-Core and empirical validation by computing students, conceptual modelers, and legal experts.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

A legal relation is a social relation typified by a legal norm and grounded in a legal fact.
`Legal Relator` specializes UFO's `Social Relator` and existentially depends on the agents or universals playing its contingent legal roles.
The taxonomy splits `Legal Relator` into `Simple Legal Relator` and `Complex Legal Relator`, although this paper develops only simple cases in prose.
A `Right-Duty Relator` binds a `Right Holder` to a `Duty Holder` through correlated right and duty moments.
Rights to omissions include non-obstruction of acts, non-disruption of characteristics or situations, and non-removal of legal positions.
Rights to acts include a right to a factual act and a right to a normative act, such as creation of a required norm.
A `NoRight-Permission Relator` binds a `Permission Holder` and `Permitter` through permission and no-right.
Permission to act means the holder has no duty not to act; permission to omit means the holder has no duty to act.
The model keeps action/omission as the object of the relation and uses examples to identify each bearer and counterparty.
Normative descriptions define rules or norms, social objects, social roles, and social-moment universals; they are not treated as identical to the positions they establish.
Agents may be human or institutional, intentionally act, and play roles such as right holder, duty holder, permission holder, or permitter only in a relational context.
The paper's triadic semantics is more informative for a KG than unary predicates such as `isPermitted`: the graph should retain holder, counterparty, action or omission, correlated position, grounding fact, and source norm.

## Evaluation & results

The only completed quantitative evidence reported is a prior systematic mapping of legal-core-ontology studies.
Of the selected studies, 45% used a Legal Positivist approach, 8% used a non-positivist legal theory, and 47% used no legal theory as a primary source.
The ontology fragment is illustrated with constitutional-right examples such as expression, correspondence, education, strike regulation, smoking, and association.
No competency-question test, reasoner run, consistency result, ontology size, task benchmark, or participant result is reported.
The proposed validation against existing domain ontologies and extension to liberties, competences, weighing, and balancing remain future work.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The paper openly describes an initial fragment and excludes protected and unprotected liberty, citizen and state competence, and Alexy's balancing system.
It models rights to something but does not yet give a complete UFO-L axiom set or operational serialization.
The claimed ontological consistency comes from grounding in UFO, but no formal consistency check or countermodel analysis is shown.
The choice of Alexy is argued normatively; the paper does not test portability across jurisdictions or theories that reject subjective rights.
Its examples largely involve citizen-state constitutional relations and do not establish coverage of private law, procedure, precedent, or institutional change.
The literature percentages are useful landscape evidence but selection counts, uncertainty, and full protocol are not presented in this paper.
Temporal validity, jurisdiction, authority, exceptions, defeasibility, conflicting interpretations, and provenance to exact text spans are absent.
Reifying every relation can improve semantic fidelity while increasing graph complexity; no query-cost or usability trade-off is evaluated.

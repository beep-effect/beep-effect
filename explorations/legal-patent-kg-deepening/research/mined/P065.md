# Ontology Patterns: Clarifying Concepts and Terminology

## Problem & positioning

Ontology reuse promises faster development and better practice, but selecting, extending, and composing reusable ontologies remains difficult.
The paper argues that “ontology design pattern” is used too broadly and obscures which development phase a pattern serves.
It aligns ontology engineering with software engineering's requirements, conceptual modeling, architecture, design, implementation, and testing distinctions.
The proposed terminology reserves `Ontology Pattern` for a recurring ontology-modeling problem plus a proven contextual solution.
Its principal move is to separate domain representation from computational design and language-specific coding.
The work is conceptual and taxonomic, supported by worked modeling examples rather than a new empirical evaluation.

## Approach / architecture

A reference domain ontology is produced during conceptual modeling for human communication, learning, problem solving, and consensus.
During design, that conceptual specification is transformed for an implementation environment and non-functional requirements such as reasoning performance.
During implementation, the design is encoded in OWL, RDFS, a DL or Datalog language, a relational database, or another operational formalism.
One reference ontology may therefore produce multiple operational ontologies with different computational tradeoffs.
The authors compare software analysis, architectural, design, and idiom patterns with ontology conceptual, architectural, design, and coding patterns.
They reclassify Gangemi and Presutti's pattern families according to the phase in which each solution applies.
They then demonstrate reuse by analogy, reuse by extension, and pattern composition using UFO-derived and domain-derived patterns.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

Ontology Conceptual Patterns are technology-independent fragments of foundational or domain reference ontologies.
Foundational Ontology Patterns (FOPs) capture general structures and are normally reused by analogy between pattern roles and the target problem.
Domain-Related Ontology Patterns (DROPs) capture core domain knowledge and are normally reused by extension through specialization and added relations.
Ontology Architectural Patterns arrange large ontologies into modules or ontology networks and may apply during conceptual modeling or early design.
Ontology Design Patterns address implementation-independent design problems; logical patterns handle representational expressivity and reasoning patterns target tractability, decidability, or performance.
Ontology Idioms are tied to a specific language or formalism; encoding n-ary semantics through classes and binaries is a logical design pattern, while doing so specifically in OWL is an idiom.
The authors exclude lexico-syntactic, presentation, and mapping practices from their strict OP definition because they do not solve a modeling problem in a new ontology-development context.
They treat many language-dependent reengineering patterns as idioms rather than general patterns.
The FOP for roles with multiple disjoint allowed types uses a role mixin over disjoint role types, ultimate kinds supplying identity, a common relational dependence, and a related type.
The commitment FOP distinguishes atomic from complex commitments; the latter compose other commitments.
The appointment FOP specializes commitment by requiring propositional content that explicitly refers to a time interval.
The Agenda Ontology composes these patterns: Contact may be Person or Organization under different identity principles, while appointments may be atomic or recurring composites.
The ROoST example extends Work Product Participation and Work Product Types DROPs into testing activities, test cases, test code, code-under-test roles, and test results.
The distinction between `Code` as a kind and `Code To Be Tested` as a role exemplifies keeping identity-bearing types separate from contextual participation.
This is not a KG construction method, but its patterns constrain the schema semantics from which reliable graph entities and edges can be instantiated.

## Evaluation & results

No new quantitative experiment, participant study, benchmark, competency-question result, or runtime measurement is reported.
The paper references prior experiments that found patterns useful and improved ontology quality/usability, but does not reproduce their numbers.
The contribution is illustrated through three FOP applications in an Agenda Ontology and two composed DROPs in a Reference Ontology on Software Testing.
The examples demonstrate that analogy may preserve a pattern's structure while changing domain labels and that extension specializes imported concepts and relations.
They also show composition: the Agenda fragment combines role, commitment, and appointment patterns; ROoST combines participation and work-product patterns.
Consequently, the evidence establishes conceptual plausibility and terminology coherence, not comparative development speed, model correctness, reuse frequency, or reasoning performance.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The authors describe ontology patterns as an approach still in its infancy and note unresolved selection, extension, and composition problems.
Their strict definition controversially excludes useful presentation, lexical, and mapping patterns rather than distinguishing modeling patterns from other reusable practices under a broader family.
The analogy to software development clarifies phases but may overstate how cleanly ontology conceptualization, design, and implementation separate in iterative practice.
“Well-proven” is part of the definition, yet the paper gives no acceptance criteria or evidence threshold for proving a pattern.
No machine-readable pattern contract specifies roles, invariants, competency questions, forbidden merges, or conformance tests.
The examples depend heavily on UFO's ontological commitments; teams using other foundational ontologies may classify identity, role, event, and commitment differently.
The taxonomy does not directly address versioning, provenance, jurisdiction, temporality, epistemic status, or conflicting source assertions.
The visual model fragments survive extraction only partially, and the prose—not diagram details—supports this distillate.

# UFO-L: A Core Ontology of Legal Concepts Built from a Legal Relations Perspective

## Problem & positioning

This doctoral-consortium paper is a research proposal, not the completed UFO-L thesis.
It identifies a persistent gap between AI/Computer Science representations and legal theory: many legal ontologies model generic legal concepts without consulting primary jurisprudential sources.
A preliminary systematic mapping found that, among 128 studies from 1995–2014, only 35 (about 27%) used primary legal-theory sources.
Another 44 studies (about 34%) used legal theory indirectly, while 49 (about 38%) used none.
The proposal argues that a theory ignoring principles as norms will yield an inflexible computational model detached from contemporary legal practice.
It chooses Alexy's Theory of Fundamental Rights, grounds the result in UFO, and calls the new legal layer UFO-L.
The conceptual shift is explicitly subjectivist: legal relations, not legal norms, become the model's center.
The proposal limits itself to the structural part of Alexy's theory and excludes dynamic legal argumentation, weighing, and balancing.

## Approach / architecture

The planned program combines a systematic mapping, documentary analysis of laws, doctrine, and jurisprudence, iterative ontology development, and empirical experiments.
UFO supplies domain-independent categories and constraints intended to reduce inconsistency, incorrectness, and incompleteness.
The relation-centered layer specializes UFO's relator, which objectifies a relational property existentially dependent on multiple individuals.
Alexy supplies rules versus principles and a system of basic legal positions; Hohfeld supplies correlated and opposite positions.
Ontology construction is planned to start with fundamental relations, then add legal facts, agents, norms, and objects.
Comparative work against CLO and LRI-Core is intended to establish what the relational perspective adds.
Three experiments are proposed: students modeling scenarios with UML and UFO-L, experienced conceptual modelers doing the same, and legal experts interpreting the resulting models.
The design aims to test clarity, correctness, consistency, coherence, completeness, reuse, and communication across law/computing roles.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The proposal defines a norm as the meaning of a normative utterance and separates deontological norms from axiological norms.
Deontological norms divide into rules, which are fulfilled or not, and principles, whose satisfaction depends on factual and legal possibilities.
A legal relation is a bond between subjects arising through a legal fact and typified in a legal norm.
Every legal position is triadic: one subject has a position against another subject regarding an object.
Alexy's basic positions include rights to something, liberties, and competences; the proposal's initial scope prioritizes the relational structure.
UFO-L is positioned above UFO-A/B/C: generic agents, roles, events, normative descriptions, and relational moments are specialized into legal categories.
The core `Legal Relator` is meant to bind legal-role bearers and their correlated positions rather than attach deontic labels directly to persons.
The preliminary relator taxonomy shown in a figure is the main model artifact, but most class names and constraints are not recoverable from the body text.
The proposal treats relations as reusable semantic infrastructure for legal-domain ontologies and interoperability, not merely as annotations on legal documents.
Its completeness hypothesis is concrete: modeling must expose every element participating in a legal relation, especially correlated positions that ordinary text leaves implicit.

## Evaluation & results

The completed result is the systematic mapping: 128 studies, with 27% using legal theory directly, 34% indirectly, and 38% not at all, subject to rounding.
The mapping also counts uses of several foundational or core ontologies in its selected literature: LRI-Core 19, LKIF 18, DOLCE 11, CLO 10, FOLaw 8, and PROTON+OPJK 8.
At publication time, the mapping and preliminary legal-relator taxonomy had been published, and a newer UFO-L model was under development.
No empirical ontology-validation result is reported in this paper.
Experiments 1–3, participant outcomes, domain case studies, and comparative results are schedules or hypotheses, not completed evidence.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The paper explicitly excludes legal argumentation and the weighing-and-balancing half of Alexy's theory.
It is a proposal-stage overview, so the relator taxonomy, axioms, competency questions, and machine-readable artifact are not detailed enough to reproduce.
The planned experiments mix subjective clarity judgments with ontological criteria but do not yet specify sample sizes, instruments, or statistical analysis.
The mapping counts establish a theory-use gap, not that UFO-L is more correct, complete, or interoperable than alternatives.
The asserted benefits—flexibility, completeness, consistency, reduced modeling cost, and better semantic interoperability—are expected outcomes here.
The approach may privilege a civil-law and fundamental-rights framing without testing common-law, procedural, contractual, or administrative settings.
It does not address temporal norm validity, jurisdiction, source authority, defeasibility, contradictory interpretations, or evidence spans.
The two-column extraction loses portions of Figure 4, so inferring the completed thesis's fourteen positions or eight patterns from this paper alone would be fabrication.

# Ontologies in the Legal Domain

## Problem & positioning

The chapter explains what legal ontologies are for and distinguishes ontological domain commitments from frameworks describing how knowledge functions in legal reasoning.
It treats ontologies as community-dependent, normative social constructs rather than neutral dictionaries.
Five application roles organize the survey: structuring information, reasoning, semantic indexing, semantic interoperability, and domain understanding.
Legal modeling is difficult because legislation is natural-language, ambiguous, vague, mutable, distributed across sources, and often only apparently coherent.
The authors stress an individuation problem: whether law is one coherent normative system or a collection of legally valid statements tied together by meta-law.
They position top ontologies as cross-domain abstractions, legal core ontologies as stable field concepts, and domain ontologies as contextual specializations.
FOLaw and LRI-Core are the main comparative cases; Dutch administrative law and CRIME.NL show applied specialization.

## Approach / architecture

The chapter synthesizes more than fifteen years of the authors' ontology and legal-knowledge-engineering work rather than reporting a controlled experiment.
FOLaw types the knowledge needed by legal reasoning and makes dependencies among those types explicit.
Its knowledge families are normative, meta-legal, world, responsibility, reactive, and creative knowledge.
Primary norms assign statuses such as permitted or prohibited to applicable behavior; meta-legal rules resolve conflicts and determine validity, applicability, and precedence.
World knowledge separates definitional/domain knowledge from causal and behavioral knowledge needed to connect norms to real situations.
Responsibility knowledge links causation, intention, blame, liability, and accountability; reactive and creative knowledge concern sanctions and the creation of legal effects.
LRI-Core instead anchors legal concepts in commonsense categories: occurrences, physical entities, mental entities, roles, and abstract entities.
Applied domain ontologies specialize these anchors while keeping legal roles distinct from the agents that bear them.
OWL DL, Protégé, and reasoners such as Pellet support formalization and classification, but the authors warn that OWL's expressiveness invites mixing ontology with epistemology.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The central modeling distinction is `ontology` versus `epistemological framework`: the former describes entities and relations; the latter describes knowledge roles and reasoning dependencies.
Top, core, and domain ontologies form a reuse stack, but meaning remains bounded by purpose and community of practice.
The chapter names inference bias: intended reasoning tasks shape formalization and therefore constrain reuse.
The administrative-law ontology starts from Administration, Stakeholder, Decision, Objection, and Request and contains about 300 classes and 170 properties.
Its top classes include Agent, Role, Act, and Legal Mental Concept, aligned to LRI-Core.
`performed-by` ranges over Role; a Role cannot act independently of an Agent, preserving the distinction between a bearer and context-dependent behavior.
Legal Act is scoped apart from factual and private-law acts; Interest, Competence, and Involvement specialize Legal Mental Concept.
LRI-Core models occurrences as event/situation particulars, distinguishes physical from mental entities, and treats roles as externally dependent and behavior-constraining.
CRIME.NL separates enduring Person identity from procedural roles such as suspect, defendant, and convict.
It centers actions: offences by alleged perpetrators and punishment actions imposed by the legal system.
Mental legal objects such as accusations and evidence may reify speech acts or epistemic roles, an explicit warning against confusing discourse objects with their underlying acts.
Norm conflicts are not automatically contradictions: intended exceptions require priority knowledge such as `lex specialis` before system-level normative status can be derived.
Legal sources omit commonsense links, so modelers must reconstruct the social domain and maintain mappings between legal and commonsense concepts.
Suggested ontology-quality lenses include path and neighborhood similarity, node centrality, internal coherence, Netsim, weighted links, OntoQA schema/population checks, and OntoClean meta-properties.

## Evaluation & results

The administrative-law case reduced an indexing vocabulary of roughly 4,000 terms through an ontology of about 300 classes and 170 properties.
The pruned vocabulary was adopted by State Council experts tagging content-management documents.
The planned ontology-backed search system had not been built, so no retrieval precision, recall, latency, or user-effectiveness result is reported.
FOLaw had practical use in multiple European ICT projects, but the authors characterize its reuse as limited and provide no quantified evaluation.
CRIME.NL illustrates conceptual coverage and LRI-Core anchoring; it is not evaluated as a populated KG or reasoning benchmark here.
The chapter reports no inter-annotator agreement, competency-question suite, axiom counts, instance counts, or controlled comparison.
It explicitly concludes that no complete, generally accepted set of intrinsic ontology-quality or ontology-similarity measures existed in the surveyed work.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The authors acknowledge that ontology meaning is context-sensitive, perspective-neutral ontology is impossible, and inference bias limits reuse.
They report difficulty keeping ontological conceptualization separate from epistemological structure in expressive OWL DL models.
OWL DL is said to be insufficient for some legal-ontology needs, but the chapter does not specify a complete requirements-to-language gap analysis.
The legal/core models reflect particular theoretical commitments and Dutch application contexts; cross-jurisdiction portability is asserted more than demonstrated.
The administrative-law outcome establishes vocabulary use, not improved retrieval quality or reduced annotation cost.
The treatment predates modern provenance, named-graph, temporal-KG, and empirical ontology-learning practices.
No explicit source-span, version, jurisdiction, validity-interval, or uncertainty model accompanies norms and interpretations.
Calling ontology concepts relatively unchangeable sits uneasily with the chapter's own account of evolving legal meaning and professional concepts.
Graph-structural metrics cannot by themselves establish legal correctness, community consensus, or fitness for a reasoning task.

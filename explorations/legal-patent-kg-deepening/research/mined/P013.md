# A Comparison of Four Ontologies for the Design of Legal Knowledge Systems

## Problem & positioning

- Legal knowledge systems necessarily embody assumptions about which legal entities and relations exist, yet those conceptualizations were often implicit.
- The paper treats an ontology as an explicit specification of a conceptualization that supports shared understanding and is distinct from any one representational formalism.
- It compares four early legal conceptualizations built for legal knowledge systems: McCarty's LLD, Stamper's NORMA, Valente's functional ontology, and Van Kralingen and Visser's frame-based ontology (FBO).
- Only LFU and FBO were proposed as legal ontologies and specified in ONTOLINGUA; LLD and NOR expose ontology-like commitments through representation formalisms.
- The goal is not to crown a winner but to reveal commitments, strengths, weaknesses, and comparison-criterion limits.
- The authors classify commitments as task, method, and domain commitments and compare artifacts with roughly shared purpose.

## Approach / architecture

- The comparison defines three top-level criteria: epistemological adequacy, operationality, and reusability.
- Epistemological adequacy decomposes into clarity, intuitiveness, relevance, completeness, and discriminative power.
- Operationality decomposes into encoding bias, coherence, and computationality.
- Reusability decomposes into task-and-method reuse and legal-subdomain reuse, including extensibility without revision.
- The authors first reconstruct each ontology's primitives, then compare them qualitatively and explicitly acknowledge subjectivity and incomplete detail.
- They also test task coverage conceptually against assessment and planning branches rather than using a common implemented benchmark.
- The analysis emphasizes purpose-relative evaluation: suitability for a particular application matters more than “good” or “bad” in the abstract.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- **LLD:** atomic predicate formulae and five rule forms support definitions, embedded implication/negation, defaults, and prototypes/deformations.
- LLD reifies predicate instances, sorts count versus mass terms, reifies temporal states, models events as state changes, and relates actors to events through actions.
- LLD supplies permitted, forbidden, obligatory, and enabled deontic operators; each may be negated.
- **NOR:** agents, behavioral invariants, and realizations model knowledge as knower- and behavior-dependent rather than truth-valued symbol manipulation.
- A NOR realization combines an agent with a behavioral invariant; operators express ability, authority, start/end, decomposition, temporal/conditional composition, and commands between agents.
- **LFU:** six legal-knowledge functions are normative, world/Legal Abstract Model, responsibility, reactive, meta-legal, and creative knowledge.
- LFU's Legal Abstract Model separates definitional knowledge from causal knowledge and mediates between commonsense world descriptions and normative knowledge.
- LFU meta-legal knowledge covers norm metadata, conflict ordering, normative defaults, and validity through empowering and derogating norms.
- **FBO:** separates a reusable legal ontology from a non-reusable statute-specific ontology.
- FBO's reusable core has `Norm`, `Act`, and `ConceptDescription` frames; norms carry source, scope, conditions, subject, modality, and act reference.
- FBO distinguishes events from processes and physical acts from institutional acts, enabling qualification of a physical occurrence as a legal event.
- Across all four, common ingredients include actions, agents, norms, definitional knowledge, and time, but the priority and granularity differ.
- LFU and FBO both model the transition from physical/social facts to institutional legal descriptions, though with different functions and status mappings.
- The paper's durable pattern is layered granularity: a reusable abstract ontology plus purpose-specific detail can balance reuse against discrimination.

## Evaluation & results

- The evaluation is a structured qualitative comparison of four ontologies, not a quantitative experiment.
- Detail is ordered from least to most discriminating as NOR, LLD, LFU, then FBO; FBO's generic-plus-statute-specific combination is most detailed.
- All four appear capable of classical assessment; support for probabilistic assessment is explicit only in FBO's factors and otherwise absent, indirect, or undeveloped.
- All four can describe cases; LLD and LFU additionally name exemplar/case-description constructs.
- LLD and FBO explicitly model state changes; NOR centers agents/actions but handles absolute time awkwardly; LFU leaves state changes underdeveloped.
- FBO was used in two operational prototypes, FRAMER-P for planning and FRAMER-A for assessment, though computational adequacy was not measured well.
- LFU was partially implemented in the ON-LINE “Legal Information Server”; implementation detail is limited.
- NOR had not been implemented, and the authors doubt its efficiency; LLD was designed with computational feasibility continually in view.
- None of the four adequately represents legal procedures, especially procedural competence norms.
- The authors find no agreed elementary legal building blocks and no gold standard for completeness or discriminative power.
- They conclude that greater detail improves discrimination but reduces arbitrary task, method, and subdomain reuse.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- Authors' own: only four conceptualizations are compared, and they are not claimed representative of every legal ontology.
- Authors' own: condensed reconstructions cannot fully capture book-length or multi-article source theories, especially NOR.
- Authors' own: comparison judgments are personal and subjective, and criteria are applied abstractly and incompletely.
- Authors' own: completeness lacks a commonly accepted theory enumerating all legal knowledge; there is no gold standard.
- Authors' own: the four systems saw little use outside their originating groups, leaving too little evidence for reliable evaluation.
- Criteria focused on legal knowledge-system developers may differ from criteria produced by legal theory or practice.
- The analysis predates modern RDF/OWL validation, linked-data interoperability, provenance standards, temporal KGs, and empirical ontology competency testing.
- No shared corpus, competency questions, inter-model mapping, reasoner workload, user study, or reproducible scoring rubric is supplied.
- The paper sometimes treats ontology and knowledge base as differing mainly by generality, which needs sharper governance in a provenance-sensitive system.
- Its deontic categories do not resolve defeasibility, jurisdictional authority, source conflict, or time-varying legal validity.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- **semantic-foundation (SKOS/@beep/ontology):** adopt the explicit purpose/commitment record and layered reusable-core plus task/domain-extension pattern; reject any claim that one abstraction level is universally ideal.
- **epistemic slice (bitemporal edges, claims, contradiction triage, evidence spans):** combine LLD/FBO event and time distinctions with source-bound institutional qualification, while treating “physical act qualifiesAs legal act” as a contestable, temporally scoped claim.
- **patent tooling / uspto-patent-driver-depth:** model patent events, actors, norms, and concept descriptions separately; use FBO-style physical/institutional act qualification for filing, publication, examination, rejection, amendment, and grant events.
- **agentic-cad-patent-tooling:** use the paper's task-fitness criteria to test drafting and planning ontology extensions with explicit competency questions; reject ontology elegance as a proxy for computational or attorney usefulness.
- **OIP solo-practice workflows:** add legal procedure as a dedicated modeled layer because all four compared ontologies underspecify it; office-action sequences and deadlines must not be squeezed into static norm classes.
- **agent memory (Cognee):** use the three-axis evaluation card—epistemological adequacy, operationality, reusability—for ontology donors, but keep legal assertions and source qualification outside the memory system of record.

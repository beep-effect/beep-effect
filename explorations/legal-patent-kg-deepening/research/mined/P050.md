# Legal Ontologies over Time: A Systematic Mapping Study

## Problem & positioning

Legal ontologies had proliferated across reasoning, search, indexing, integration, and legal-action simulation without a consolidated classification of reusable work.
The paper responds with a systematic mapping study intended to characterize the field, reveal trends and gaps, and reduce reinvention.
It categorizes work by publication, purpose, legal theory, domain, generalization level, engineering method, evaluation, legal-text peculiarities, anomalies, and formalization standard.
The mapping is intentionally broad rather than a deep meta-analysis of effect sizes.
Its reuse thesis is that organizing existing ontologies can enable prefabricated models to be coupled into more complex practical legal systems.

## Approach / architecture

The protocol has planning, conduction, and packaging phases and defines ten research questions.
Searches cover ACM, IEEE, Springer, Scopus, Web of Science, Engineering Village/Compendex, and ScienceDirect.
Search terms combine variants of ontology with legal/law/juridical and domain/concept/norm/code/knowledge/information/document/text/reasoning terms.
Two reviewers apply inclusion and exclusion criteria using separate forms for included and excluded studies.
Selection proceeds through automated title/abstract/keyword search, triad screening, and full-text screening.
Included studies must propose or apply a legal ontology, be peer-reviewed, be in English, and be electronically accessible.
The classification distinguishes ontology purposes, top/general/core/domain/task/application levels, legal theories, engineering strategies, evaluation approaches, and representation standards.
Engineering strategies are top-down, bottom-up, hybrid, and middle-out.
Evaluation categories include gold-standard/conceptual analysis, case-based, data-driven, and user/expert-based approaches.
Legal-text findings are separated into sources of difficulty and observed syntactic or semantic anomalies.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

The paper provides a taxonomy of ontology purposes: organization/structuring, reasoning/problem solving, semantic indexing/search, semantic integration/interoperation, and domain understanding.
It identifies Hart, Hohfeld, and Kelsen as the dominant legal-theory foundations, with Alexy, intentional, and negotiation theories appearing less often.
Generalization levels distinguish upper/foundational, core, domain, task, and application ontologies.
Legislative modeling splits into structural/formal profiles—norms, articles, paragraphs, sections—and semantic profiles representing normative provisions.
The map identifies five upper legal ontologies and numerous core models, including FOLaw, LKIF/LRI-Core, CLO, and UFO-L.
UFO-L is classified around legal relations; other core models emphasize judicial acts, agents/events/roles, deontic modalities, or generic legal knowledge.
Twelve mapped subdomains include traffic, tax, civil, local government, labor, succession, commercial, contractual, consumer, criminal, privacy, and license law.
Intellectual-property examples include law-article, international-copyright, digital-rights-management, and technical/legal integration ontologies.
Five recurring sources of anomalies are writing style, heterogeneous sources, information volume, normative dynamicity, and overlapping jurisdictions.
Syntactic anomalies include incompleteness, conflicting/discrepant norms, and dense cross-references.
Semantic anomalies include polysemy, synonymy/near-synonymy, inconsistency, under-specification, open texture, and inaccurate modeling.
The authors argue that logical formalization can expose inconsistent definitions and subsumption errors, but defeasible logic is needed for exceptions and principle/rule conflicts.
RDF/OWL is the dominant formalization pair, used in more than half of the selected studies; UML, first-order logic, ORM, COREL, and PAL also appear.

## Evaluation & results

The initial search returns 4,315 records: 38 ACM, 32 IEEE, 3,167 Springer, 584 Scopus, 313 Compendex, 16 ScienceDirect, and 165 Web of Science.
Title/abstract/keyword screening retains 193 records, 4.47% of the initial set.
Full-text screening yields 78 primary studies.
Publication types are 25 journal articles, 36 conference papers, 13 workshop papers, and 4 book chapters.
Approximately half of the mapped studies appeared in the then-current decade.
Reasoning and problem solving accounts for 41% of studies; understanding the domain accounts for 12%.
Forty-four studies, about 56% overall and 88% of theory-aligned studies, use the Hart/Hohfeld/Kelsen positivist cluster.
Thirty-six percent do not align the ontology with an explicit legal-philosophical basis.
Core ontologies account for 46% and domain ontologies 51% of the selected studies; only one application ontology is reported.
Seventy-three of 78 ontologies were manually engineered; the discussion summarizes this as 93.5%.
Engineering approaches include 14 bottom-up, 12 top-down, 9 hybrid, and 2 middle-out studies.
Thirty-two of 78 studies—41%—report no ontology verification or validation activity.
Only 3.8% are reported as having specialist support in ontology evaluation.
Thirty-eight selected studies identify syntactic or semantic anomalies.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

The authors identify threats from search-string formulation, search coverage, and data extraction.
Using only the standard term “ontology” improves precision but can miss work labeled conceptual model, semantic model, vocabulary, or knowledge graph.
English-only and electronically accessible criteria exclude potentially important jurisdiction-specific work.
Springer’s limited field filtering generates 3,167 initial hits and may create uneven screening effort across sources.
The mapping’s category assignments are sometimes non-disjoint, so percentages do not always describe mutually exclusive populations.
The “gold standard” evaluation bucket combines foundational-ontology grounding, conceptual-analysis methods, and explicit comparison, which are not equivalent kinds of validation.
The corpus ends before recent LLM/GraphRAG systems, modern legal-KG benchmarks, and newer legal standards; its trend findings are historical rather than current.
Counts establish prevalence, not ontology quality, real-world adoption, maintenance fitness, or inference correctness.
The study does not publish a machine-actionable crosswalk from each ontology’s concrete classes and relations to the classification dimensions in the article text.
Its call for reuse needs versioning, licensing, jurisdiction, temporal validity, and alignment evidence that the mapping itself does not supply.

## Beep-relevance hooks

- semantic-foundation (SKOS/@beep/ontology): use the paper’s purpose/generalization/theory dimensions as required ontology metadata; reject importing a core model without documenting its legal-theory commitments.
- epistemic slice: directly model the five anomaly sources plus incompleteness, discrepancy, cross-reference, ambiguity, and open texture as claim-review triggers with bitemporal norm versions.
- patent tooling (uspto-patent-driver-depth): survey existing IP/copyright/license ontologies before inventing patent concepts, but re-evaluate them for current USPTO entities, temporal events, and source provenance.
- patent tooling (agentic-cad-patent-tooling): require competency questions, case-based tests, and expert review; the mapped 41% validation gap is a concrete anti-pattern.
- OIP solo-practice workflows: distinguish structural document markup from substantive legal interpretation so document navigation cannot masquerade as legal reasoning.
- agent memory (Cognee): attach purpose, abstraction level, theory, jurisdiction, method, and validation status to ingested ontology assets to prevent false interchangeability.

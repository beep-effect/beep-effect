# A Systematic Mapping of the Literature on Legal Core Ontologies

## Problem & positioning

- Legal-ontology publications had proliferated, but the generic legal core ontology (LCO) subfield lacked a systematic overview.
- The paper distinguishes core ontologies from legal domain, task, and application ontologies under a Guarino-style classification.
- It asks which LCO studies exist, which contribution niches they occupy, which legal theories they use, and which foundational/core ontologies ground them.
- The authors are motivated by perceived overreliance on positivist theory and by the scarcity of foundationally grounded legal ontologies.
- The mapping is intended to identify gaps and inputs for a future unified LCO, not to establish one ontology as correct.
- Its stated headline is that few studies use legal theories suited to contemporary societal challenges.

## Approach / architecture

- The review follows planning, search, screening, keywording/classification, and data-extraction phases.
- Scope: English papers and book chapters from 1995–2014 in Computer Science/Computer and Law, focused on LCOs and generic legal concepts.
- Inclusion requires references to terms such as legal theory, legal concepts, legal relation, legal fact, or Hohfeldian classification.
- Exclusions cover abstract-only material, presentations, technical reports, duplicates and superseded versions, non-English studies, and Law/Philosophy-only work.
- Google Scholar is the single bibliographic source.
- A control group is used to revise the search string toward both known-paper recall and broader study capture.
- Selected papers are classified by contribution—language, method, tool, model—and by their use of legal theory and foundational ontology.
- The inspection fields are abstracts, keywords, introductions, and references rather than full formal reconstruction of every ontology.

## Ontology & KG modeling contributions

- The paper makes ontology scope a first-class classification: core concepts reusable across law differ from domain-, task-, and application-specific commitments.
- It identifies an LCO lineage including FOLaw, Kralingen's ontology, CLO, NM-L/NM-core, LRI-Core, Legal-RDF, PROTON+OPJK, OPLK, LKIF-core, LegalRuleML-core, and LOTED core.
- Legal theories are grouped as Legal Positivism, Inclusive Positivism, Legal Realism, Legal Interpretivism, and Legal Argumentation.
- Theoretical provenance is differentiated as primary legal-theory use, indirect use through another LCO, or absence of primary sourcing.
- Foundational/core anchors reported include LKIF, LKIF-core, LRI-CORE, SUMO, DOLCE, CLO, FOLAW, and OPJK.
- Contribution-kind facets—language, method, tool, model—help separate a representation syntax or application from the conceptual ontology it consumes.
- The analysis finds that many artifacts presented under generic “legal ontology” language actually model specific fragments of law.
- Hohfeld is a recurring source for claim, duty, obligation, permission, and legal-relation modeling, partly because of the classification's logic-based form.
- The authors argue that positivist theory alone omits social-reality concepts needed for newer legal problems, motivating explicit theory selection rather than invisible inheritance.

## Evaluation & results

- Search produces 269 studies; application of the screening criteria leaves 128.
- The source reports that LCO publication activity peaks in 2005–2009 and remains substantial in 2010–2014.
- The publication chart's numeric labels are not present in this artifact's extracted text and are therefore not reported here.
- After excluding reviews and opinion papers, this artifact's prose says 116 studies were classified by contribution.
- Figure 3's category labels are recoverable, but its bar values are absent from `pdftotext`; no values are inferred from the companion artifact.
- Among 128 selected works, 35 (about 27%) use primary sources of legal theory, 44 (about 34%) use indirect sources, and 49 (about 38%) use no primary source.
- The study identifies 47 papers proposing a kind of ontology; 32% of those do not ground it in a foundational/core ontology.
- Exactly 16 selected studies refer directly to Hohfeld, with others using it indirectly.
- Legal Positivism is reported as the most frequently used theory, while models are reported as the most extensively populated contribution niche.
- The analysis concludes that genuine LCOs remain few because many seemingly generic proposals are domain ontologies.

## Limitations (authors' own + yours)

- Authors' own: excluding generic search terms “legal ontology” and “legal ontologies” creates false-negative risk for papers that use those labels for LCOs.
- Authors' own: synonymous phrasing such as “concept of law” can evade the selected theory/concept terms.
- Authors' own: the search string naturally favors ontology models; studying languages, methods, tools, and applications comprehensively requires different terms.
- The review covers only 1995–2014 and cannot establish the current legal-ontology landscape.
- Reliance on Google Scholar alone and lack of a frozen search export reduce reproducibility.
- No inter-rater agreement, duplicate independent screening, study-quality appraisal, or ontology-level conformance testing is reported.
- This artifact's prose total of 116 contribution studies cannot be checked against Figure 3 because its numeric bar labels were not text-extracted.
- A companion local artifact, P011, says 115 and exposes labels totaling 115; that external artifact-level discrepancy must not be silently reconciled.
- Named foundational grounding does not prove correct axioms, usable mappings, maintained identifiers, or fit for legal-practice tasks.
